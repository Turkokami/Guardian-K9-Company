## Who is actually responsible for bed bugs in a Washington rental?

The landlord, in most cases, with two exceptions that matter enormously.

**RCW 59.18.060(4)** requires a landlord to "provide a reasonable program for the
control of infestation by insects, rodents, and other pests at the initiation of the
tenancy and, except in the case of a single-family residence, control infestation
during tenancy except where such infestation is caused by the tenant."

Read that twice, because two carve-outs are buried in one sentence. A single-family
residence is treated differently from a multi-unit building. And an infestation caused
by the tenant sits outside the landlord's ongoing duty. **RCW 59.18.130** is the other
half of the picture: it sets out tenant duties, and it is the provision behind tenant
liability for extermination costs where the tenant caused the problem.

So the statute does not answer the question you are actually asking. It tells you the
duty is yours unless the tenant caused it, and then leaves you to establish whether the
tenant caused it. That is an evidentiary problem, not a legal one, and it is where most
property managers lose money. You can be entirely right about causation and still lose
the chargeback because you cannot show your working.

I will say plainly what a dog cannot do here. A detection dog cannot tell you who
brought bed bugs into a building. Nobody's dog can. What a screening programme gives you
is distribution and timing: which units were searched, on which date, which alerted,
which were confirmed, and which were clear. That is the raw material of a causation
argument. It is not the argument itself.

There is more on this in our note on
[who is responsible under Washington landlord-tenant law](/compliance/washington-landlord-tenant-bed-bugs/).

## What does Washington actually require you to disclose?

Less than people think, and it is worth being precise because the misinformation here is
persistent.

There is no statewide statute in this state requiring a landlord to disclose a prior
infestation to a prospective tenant. I have seen that claim made confidently in trade
articles and in vendor marketing, sometimes attached to a bill number. The bill usually
cited is HB 1440 from 2019, which concerns 60-day notice for rent increases and has
nothing to do with pests at all.

I mention it because a vendor who will invent a statute to close a sale will invent other
things too, and because building your internal policy on a requirement that does not
exist is a poor use of your time. What does bind you is the habitability duty above, the
entry rules below, and whatever your local jurisdiction adds on top.

## How do Seattle's rules differ from the rest of the state?

Seattle is the jurisdiction that names bed bugs explicitly, and it attaches real money to
the naming.

Under **SMC 22.206.160.A.3** and **SMC 22.206.170.C**, owners of rental housing in
Seattle must control insects, and bed bugs are named specifically rather than swept into
a general pest category. **SDCI Director's Rule 2-88** sets out the standards an
extermination programme is expected to meet and the verification procedures around it.
Penalties run from **$150 to $500 per day**, plus inspection charges. The Rental
Registration and Inspection Ordinance subjects registered rentals to periodic inspection
on a cycle.

Outside Seattle, most of the state runs on RCW 59.18.060(4) alone. Pierce County, for
instance, has no Seattle-style municipal provision naming bed bugs. That contrast catches
regional managers out constantly, because a policy written for a Seattle portfolio
assumes a documentation standard the rest of the portfolio is not being held to, and a
policy written for Tacoma under-documents the Seattle assets.

We keep a fuller summary at
[Seattle rental bed bug rules and penalties](/compliance/seattle-rental-bed-bug-rules/).

## What does Bellingham's rental registration programme mean for your documentation?

If you hold property in Bellingham, you are already inside an inspection culture, and
almost nobody writes about what that means for pest documentation.

Bellingham operates a Rental Registration and Safety Inspection Program under **BMC
6.15**. Rentals must be registered before they are let. A certificate of registration has
to be posted in each unit where it can be seen. Units are inspected on roughly a three to
three-and-a-half year cycle, and owners may use either a City inspector or a qualified
private inspector who has passed the programme's training and holds the required
credential.

The programme is about life and fire safety rather than pests. But the operational
consequence is that Bellingham owners are used to a third-party inspector walking their
units on a schedule, to producing a certificate on demand, and to a compliance file that
somebody outside the company might read. A dated detection report drops into that habit
without friction. In markets with no registration programme, the same document often
arrives as an unfamiliar object and has to be explained before it can be used.

This is one of several reasons our schedule is densest in the north. Whatcom and Skagit
are the prime work zones, and you can read the specifics for
[Bellingham](/service-area/bellingham/) and for
[Mount Vernon](/service-area/mount-vernon/), the Skagit county seat.

## Why does an independent inspection resolve a dispute that an in-house one cannot?

Because of who is paying, and what they are paid for.

When the company that inspects your building is also the company that sells you the
treatment, the inspection has a built-in incentive to find work. That is not an
accusation against any particular firm. It is a structural fact about how the transaction
is arranged, and everybody in the room understands it, including your tenant and
including their attorney.

Guardian is arranged the other way round. We do not sell treatment, we do not apply
pesticides, and we do not take a referral fee from anyone who does. When we confirm a
problem, you engage a licensed applicator of your own choosing, and we have no interest
in which one. When we find nothing, that finding costs us the follow-on work we would
have earned if we had found something, which is precisely why it is worth something.

The awkward corollary is that we are a cost with no upsell attached. A treatment company
can price an inspection at nothing because the inspection is a lead. We cannot, and we
would rather explain that than pretend our pricing is competitive with a loss leader.

## When one unit is confirmed, which neighbours do you screen?

More than one, and the right answer depends on construction rather than on a rule of
thumb.

The convention of screening the units left, right, above and below comes from stacked
mid-rise construction where those are the shared assemblies. It transfers badly. In
garden-style buildings of the kind that dominate a great deal of Washington's
workforce housing, units frequently share attic space and utility chases that do not
follow the neat cross pattern, and the unit that matters may be one you would not have
listed. In older wood-frame stock, trim, baseboard and wall voids offer routes and
harbourage that sealed modern assemblies do not.

So we ask what the building is before we ask which units to search. Then we search a
wider set than the minimum, because the cost of screening one extra unit is small and the
cost of missing the unit that reseeds the building is not.

and the mechanics of doing it at scale are covered under
[building-wide sweeps](/services/building-wide-sweep/).

## What does entry notice require before a building-wide sweep?

Proper notice, served far enough in advance that it is actually proper.

**RCW 59.18.150** governs a landlord's right of entry and the notice that goes with it.
For a single unit this is routine. For a sweep of forty or four hundred units it is a
logistics exercise, and it is the part that most often derails a schedule. We book
building-wide work far enough out that notices can be issued correctly, because a sweep
where a third of the doors do not open is not a sweep. It is a partial search with a
misleading map at the end of it, and a misleading map is worse than no map.

Practically, that means we want your access plan before we want your calendar. Who is
serving notice. Who holds keys. Whether there are units with access restrictions or
residents who need particular handling. Whether anyone needs an interpreter. Getting this
right is unglamorous and it is most of the difference between a programme that works and
one that produces a document nobody trusts.

The notice rules are set out at
[entry notice for unit inspections](/compliance/entry-notice-for-unit-inspections/).

## What is in the report, and who signs it?

A dated document, signed by the handler who did the work, describing what was searched
and what was found, including where nothing was found.

The alert map is the core of it: every location the dog alerted on, recorded against the
unit and the position within the unit. Next to each alert is the visual confirmation
result, in one of three states. Confirmed, meaning a live insect, cast skins, eggs or
faecal staining were seen and photographed. Not confirmed, meaning the dog alerted and
nothing was visually found. Inconclusive, meaning access or conditions prevented a proper
look.

That middle category is the one that gets quietly deleted on other people's reports, and
we keep it. An unconfirmed alert is real information. It might be residual odour from a
past infestation, it might be a scent source in an adjoining void, it might be the dog
being wrong. Recording it honestly is what makes the confirmed findings credible. A
report where every single alert was confirmed should make you suspicious rather than
satisfied.

Where nothing is found at all, you get a written all-clear that states what was searched
and, just as importantly, what was not. We retain the file so it can be reissued later,
because the request almost always arrives months after the visit, usually from somebody's
lawyer.

## How accurate is canine detection, honestly?

More variable than the industry says, and the variability is the useful part.

You have probably seen a very high accuracy figure quoted in this market. It comes from
controlled laboratory work with planted hides. The field research, conducted in naturally
infested apartments, produced substantially lower average detection and a wide spread
between teams. The full numbers, the citations and what they mean are set out on our
[detection accuracy](/detection-accuracy/) page, and I would rather send you there than
put a flattering figure in a sales paragraph.

The productive way to read that spread is this: the range between the best and worst
teams is enormous, which means *the team* is the variable, not the species. A dog is not a
technology you buy off a shelf with a fixed specification. It is a working animal whose
performance depends on training, maintenance, handler skill and how the search is run.

That is why we publish the handler, the dog, the certifying body and the certification
dates rather than a percentage. When those details are filled in on this site they will
appear on every page at once, and until they are, no certification is claimed anywhere.
A credential you cannot check is not a credential.

## What does a programme cadence look like across a portfolio?

A schedule, a consistent document, and a file that builds.

Most operators of any size move from call-outs to a cadence within a year of starting,
because the economics change once you count vacancy. Statewide apartment vacancy was
around 5.5% in the third quarter of 2025, and around 5.6% across Puget Sound, with King
County average rent at $2,126 according to the UW Runstad Center's quarterly reporting. A
unit held out of service while a dispute is argued has a daily price attached, and that
price is usually larger than the screening that would have prevented the argument.

The scale of the exposure is worth stating plainly. Roughly 36% of Washington households
rent, which is on the order of 1.1 million renter households statewide. In Seattle the
proportion is far higher: about 56% of households rent, something like 205,000 renter
households, in a city Orkin placed 30th on its 2026 bed bug cities list, up from 39th the
year before. That ranking measures Orkin's own treatment volume rather than scientific
incidence, and should be read as a demand signal rather than an epidemiological one.

A workable cadence usually combines three things. Turnover screening on units as they
flip, which is the cheapest moment to find a problem. Periodic screening of a rotating
sample of occupied units, which is what turns a set of incidents into a baseline.
Verification after any treatment event, which is what closes a file rather than leaving it
open. Those map to [turnover screening](/services/turnover-screening/),
[building-wide sweeps](/services/building-wide-sweep/) and
[post-treatment verification](/services/bed-bug-verification-inspection/).
