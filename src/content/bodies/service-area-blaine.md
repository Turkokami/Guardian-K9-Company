## Why is Blaine a different problem from the rest of the county?

Because a much larger share of the beds here are slept in by people who do not live in them.

Most of Whatcom is conventional housing with residents on leases. Blaine has an unusually
high proportion of vacation accommodation, second homes and short-stay lodging relative to
its permanent population. Semiahmoo and Birch Bay run on a resort and holiday rhythm.
Two border crossings put a steady flow of travellers through the town. And a meaningful part
of the housing stock sits empty for long stretches and then fills.

That inverts the usual risk arithmetic. In a conventional apartment building the occupant is
constant and introduction is comparatively rare. Here the unit is constant and the occupant
changes repeatedly, which is the pattern that produces most of the introduction opportunity
in this industry.

We work Blaine on the regular schedule. It is the far north of our prime zone rather than
the far end of somebody else's route, and that distinction is the practical reason a
northern operator can offer a real date here.

## What does the turnover rate actually change?

Everything about when to look, and very little about how.

A unit let on a twelve-month lease has a handful of introduction opportunities a year. A
vacation rental changing hands weekly through a season has dozens. The search itself is
identical. What differs is that in a high-turnover property, waiting for somebody to
complain means waiting for the one guest in many who both reacts to bites and is willing to
say so, and by then the unit has hosted a long sequence of other people.

The other structural problem is that nobody sees the same room often enough to notice a
change. In a hotel, housekeeping works the same rooms repeatedly and develops a sense of
what normal looks like. In scattered vacation rentals cleaned by different people on
different schedules, that institutional memory does not exist.

So the useful pattern here is calendar-driven rather than complaint-driven. Screen before an
occupancy season starts, and again after it ends. That is when a unit can actually be held
if something is found, which is the whole practical constraint. Screening in the middle of
August is when you least want an answer.

The setting is covered at [hotels and lodging](/commercial/hotels-and-lodging/), and the
mechanics between occupancies at
[move-in and move-out screening](/services/turnover-screening/).

## What about the second homes?

They carry a profile that does not appear anywhere else in our service area.

A property that sits unoccupied for months and is then used intensively for a few weeks
behaves differently from both a rental and a permanent residence. Problems introduced at the
end of one occupancy period can sit undisturbed through a long vacancy and be discovered all
at once by the next occupants, who reasonably assume the problem is current rather than
months old.

Long vacancy does not resolve an infestation on any timescale an owner should plan around.
It is also worth knowing that the absence of any complaints during a vacancy is not
evidence of anything at all, because there was nobody there to complain.

For an owner or a management company handling second homes, the practical advice is that the
inspection worth buying is the one before the season rather than the one after the phone
call. It is cheaper, the unit is empty and fully accessible, and a dated all-clear at the
start of an occupancy period is a genuinely useful document if a guest raises something
later.

## Do the border crossings matter?

They shape the traveller mix rather than creating a distinct hazard.

Blaine hosts the Peace Arch passenger crossing and the Pacific Highway commercial crossing,
which put a high volume of cross-border travellers and long-haul drivers through local
accommodation. Long-haul drivers in particular move between short-stay accommodation
repeatedly, which is the high-mobility occupancy pattern that matters, and it is the same
pattern that makes contractor lodging around Cherry Point in
[Ferndale](/service-area/ferndale/) worth screening on a cadence.

I will not attach a figure to that. There is no dataset I have seen measuring introduction
rates by crossing volume, and manufacturing one would undercut everything else this site
argues. The defensible statement is narrower: accommodation serving high-mobility travellers
sees more introduction opportunity per room-night than housing serving settled residents,
and that is a scheduling argument rather than an alarming one.

## What governs a rental here?

State law, unmodified.

**RCW 59.18.060(4)** places the duty to control infestation on the landlord, with the two
carve-outs that decide most disputes: single-family residences, and infestations caused by
the tenant. **RCW 59.18.130** is the route to tenant cost liability where causation is
established. **RCW 59.18.150** requires at least two days written notice to enter and
inspect a tenanted unit.

Blaine adds no municipal provision naming bed bugs. Neither does most of Washington outside
Seattle, where they are named explicitly and penalties run from $150 to $500 per day.

There is a wrinkle specific to this market worth flagging. Much of the accommodation here is
short-stay rather than tenancy, and landlord-tenant law is not the governing framework for a
hotel room or a nightly vacation let. The obligations there come from a different direction
entirely, and the commercial driver is nightly revenue rather than habitability duty. Mixed
portfolios containing both long-term rentals and short-stay units are therefore operating
under two different sets of rules at once, which catches people out. The tenancy side is
summarised at
[who is responsible under Washington landlord-tenant law](/compliance/washington-landlord-tenant-bed-bugs/).

## What does the report do for a short-stay operator?

Closes a complaint that would otherwise be unanswerable.

The characteristic problem in vacation rentals and lodging is a guest asserting they were
bitten, with no way for anybody to confirm or refute it. Bites are not a diagnosis:
reactions vary enormously between people, a substantial share of people show no visible
reaction at all, and plenty of other things bite or irritate skin. Without an inspection
record, the operator is in the worst possible position, unable to defend the unit or to act
on it with confidence.

A dated search with a written result changes that in both directions. Where nothing is
found, you have a document from an independent party stating what was searched, on what
date, and that nothing was present, which is a materially stronger response than an
assurance. Where something is found, you know before the review is written rather than
after.

Both outcomes are worth the visit, and the second is not a failure of the service. What the
report will not do is prove absence in general or establish who brought something in, and we
would rather say that than let a document be relied on for more than it can carry. The
standard is at
[what makes a report defensible](/compliance/what-a-defensible-report-contains/).

## What does the housing stock change about scoping?

Furnished units make the search slower and more thorough at the same time.

A vacation rental is fully furnished by definition, and the furnishings are the target: beds
and headboards, upholstered seating and its underside, seams and piping, and any built-in or
fitted furniture. That is more to search than an empty long-let unit between tenancies, and
it takes longer per unit.

The offsetting advantage is access. Between guests there is no occupant, no notice
requirement, no pet to secure and nothing personal in the way, so almost none of the unit
ends up recorded as inconclusive. Coverage in a changeover window is usually better than
coverage in an occupied apartment, which makes the results more useful as baseline data.

The scheduling constraint is the changeover window itself, which is short and immovable. We
would rather work around your pattern than ask you to hold a unit empty. Tell us your
changeover day and we will build the visit around it.

## What should a changeover clean be looking for?

Four things, and none of them require a dog.

Your cleaners are in every unit, every changeover, more often than any inspector will ever
be. They are the cheapest detection capacity you have and most operators never tell them what
to look at.

**Dark spotting along seams.** Not crumbs sitting on the surface, but small dark marks that
have bled slightly into the fabric, clustered along mattress seams and piping, at the
junction where skirting meets flooring, and in the folds of upholstered furniture. This is
the most commonly found evidence in practice.

**Cast skins.** Translucent, hollow shells the shape of the insect itself, usually tucked into
a crevice. They persist long after the insect has gone and indicate a population that was
breeding rather than a single traveller carried in.

**Live insects.** About the size of an apple seed, flat, reddish-brown, rounder and darker
after feeding. Immature stages are smaller, paler and genuinely hard to see against light
fabric.

**The headboard.** Specifically the back of it and the wall behind, which is the single most
commonly skipped surface in any changeover and one of the most productive places to look.

What to do about a find is simple: photograph it, note the unit and date, do not clean it
away, and tell whoever makes the decisions. Cleaning removes the evidence that would let
somebody confirm what it was. And a report from a cleaner is a reason to inspect, not a
reason to book treatment; plenty of dark specks are not what they look like.

None of this replaces a screening cadence, and it is not a substitute for a search that
covers voids and assemblies nobody can open. It just means the cheapest observer you have is
looking at the right surfaces.

## What do we need before we arrive?

Three things, and scattered rentals make the third one matter more than usual.

**The unit list with addresses.** Vacation rentals are rarely in one building, and travel
between properties is real time that has to be priced. Several units in one visit costs
considerably less per unit than the same units booked piecemeal.

**Access, unit by unit.** Lockbox codes, key handover, or a named person per property. This
is where scattered portfolios go wrong: one missing code turns a tight changeover window into
a wasted trip.

**Your changeover pattern.** Which days, and how long the gap is. We build the visit around
your window rather than asking you to hold a unit empty, but that only works if we know the
window before the date is set.
