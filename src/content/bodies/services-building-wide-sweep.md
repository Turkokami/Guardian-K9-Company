## What problem is a sweep actually solving?

Not "is there a problem in this unit," but "how far has this gone."

The moment a second unit is confirmed in a building, the question changes shape. One unit is
an incident. Two units is a distribution, and a distribution cannot be understood by
inspecting units one at a time as complaints arrive, because complaints arrive from the
people who react to bites and notice them. A substantial share of people show no visible
reaction at all, which means complaint-driven discovery systematically under-reports and
does so unevenly across a building.

A sweep replaces that with a survey. Every unit searched in sequence over a defined period,
each result recorded against a unit number and a date, producing one document describing the
state of the whole property on a known day. That is a fundamentally different object from a
stack of individual inspection reports accumulated over eight months, and it supports
decisions the stack cannot.

It also tends to be cheaper than the alternative, which is chasing an infestation around a
building for a year while treating units reactively as each one surfaces.

## When is it worth doing?

Five situations, and the cost case is different in each.

**More than one confirmed unit.** The clearest trigger. Two confirmations in a shared-wall
building means the relevant question is about the building.

**Recurring complaints in the same building.** Where the same property generates reports
every few months and each one is handled individually, there is usually a reservoir
somewhere that unit-by-unit response keeps missing. A sweep finds reservoirs; reactive
inspection finds symptoms.

**Taking over a property.** New management, new ownership, or a portfolio acquisition where
the pest history is undocumented or simply unavailable. A baseline sweep in the first months
converts an unknown liability into a dated record, and it is far easier to establish what
you inherited than to argue about it two years later.

**Closing out a treatment programme.** After remediation across multiple units, a sweep
establishes independently whether the building is actually clear. An operator verifying its
own work has an obvious difficulty here, which is why this is one of the most common reasons
we are engaged.

**Before a sale or a refinance.** A dated third-party survey of the whole property is a
materially stronger position than an assurance, in either direction. If the building is
clean, you can show it. If it is not, you would rather know before somebody else finds out.

## How does a sweep actually run?

In layers, and the layering is what makes it finish on schedule.

**Layer one: no notice required.** Common areas, vacant units, management offices, laundry
rooms, shared lounges and corridors. No occupant means no entry notice question and no
scheduling negotiation. These can be worked while notices for the occupied units are still
running their statutory period, which is why a well-planned sweep often begins before the
main day.

**Layer two: occupied units.** These set the lead time. Entry notice under
**RCW 59.18.150** requires at least two days written notice, and that is a floor rather than
a schedule. In practice you also need a current unit list, notices drafted and served, and
somebody reachable to answer resident questions before the date.

**Layer three: the units that did not open.** There are always some. Decide in advance
whether a second pass is worth arranging or whether they will simply be recorded as not
searched, because deciding on the day produces a worse outcome than deciding in the plan.

Within each occupied unit the search runs the same way it does anywhere: perimeter first,
then furniture, then the high-probability assemblies — bed frames and headboards,
upholstered seating, seams, and the junction where skirting meets floor. When the dog
indicates, the search stops and the handler examines the location directly before anything
is recorded.

The access constraint is set out in full at
[entry notice for unit inspections](/compliance/entry-notice-for-unit-inspections/).

## Why is the access plan more important than the calendar?

Because a sweep is limited by doors, not by search speed.

This is the part clients consistently underestimate. The dog is fast. Search time in a
standard occupied one-bedroom is a matter of minutes. What governs the day is how many units
can actually be entered, and that is determined weeks earlier by decisions nobody thought of
as inspection decisions.

The failure pattern is consistent enough to describe precisely. A manager books a sweep for
the following week. Notices go out slightly late, or to a stale rent roll, or to unit numbers
that no longer match occupancy. On the day, a third of the doors do not open. What comes out
the other end is not a building sweep. It is a partial survey producing a document that
looks comprehensive, which is worse than not having run it, because now there is a report
understating its own coverage.

So we ask for the access plan before the date. Who is admitting us. Who holds keys. Which
units have restricted access. Which residents need particular handling. Whether anyone needs
the notice in another language, which matters a great deal in agricultural workforce housing
across Whatcom and Skagit and in a good deal of urban multifamily. Get those answered and
the sweep is straightforward. Leave them and the day gets half-finished.

## What comes out at the end?

One document covering the whole building, including the parts that went badly.

Every unit appears with a result. Every alert location is recorded against the unit and the
position within it, with a visual confirmation result in one of three states.

**Confirmed** means the handler saw live insects, cast skins, eggs or faecal staining at the
alert location and photographed it where possible. **Not confirmed** means the dog alerted
and nothing was visually found; it stays in the report as an alert and is never promoted
into a finding. **Inconclusive** means access, clutter or conditions prevented a proper look.

And every unit that could not be entered is listed by number as not searched.

That last item is the one that separates a useful survey from a flattering one. A report
covering forty-four units of a fifty-unit building, with no mention of the other six, invites
a reader in two years to conclude the building was screened. It was not. The gap matters
most in exactly the scenario where the report gets read, which is when something surfaces
later in one of the six.

The standard the document is held to is at
[what makes a report defensible](/compliance/what-a-defensible-report-contains/).

## What does the map let you do that a list does not?

Direct the response, and argue about causation from evidence rather than assumption.

A building-wide alert map shows clustering. Confirmed findings on one stack, or along one
corridor, or in units sharing a utility chase, describe a pattern that individual reports
delivered months apart never reveal. That pattern is what tells a licensed applicator where
to concentrate, and it makes their scoping more accurate and their quote more honest.

It also changes the responsibility conversation. Under **RCW 59.18.060(4)** the duty to
control infestation sits with the landlord except in a single-family residence and except
where the tenant caused it, and **RCW 59.18.130** is the route to tenant liability where
causation is established. Causation is an evidentiary question, and a building-wide
distribution is evidence of a kind that a single-unit inspection cannot produce.

Be careful about what it does not establish. No inspection determines origin. A dog cannot
tell you who introduced bed bugs into a building, and a map showing the heaviest confirmation
in one unit is not proof that the unit is the source. Distribution and timing are what you
get. The argument is built from them; it is not handed to you. The statutory picture is at
[who is responsible under Washington landlord-tenant law](/compliance/washington-landlord-tenant-bed-bugs/).

## How do you keep residents on side?

By telling them what is actually happening, which most operators do not.

The single highest-value thing in a sweep is the notice, and not for statutory reasons. A
resident who receives a bare legal notice of entry assumes the worst: that they are being
inspected, that they may be blamed, that they may be charged, that they should hide things
or clean frantically.

A notice that explains a trained dog will walk through the rooms, that no chemicals are
applied and nothing is treated, that the visit takes minutes, and that nobody is being
accused, removes almost all of that. Add one more sentence and it removes more: tell people
not to deep clean, launder bedding, strip the bed or move furniture beforehand. Those are
the instinctive preparations and every one of them makes the search worse by removing what
the handler would otherwise confirm visually.

There is a longer-run effect worth knowing. Buildings with a reputation for blaming residents
have measurably worse access rates, and the cost of that lands on the owner as inconclusive
entries. Buildings we have swept before are easier to sweep again, because residents have
learned the visit is short, undramatic and not an accusation.

## How does a large property get sequenced?

By floor or stack rather than by unit number, and across fewer days than people assume.

The instinct is to work numerically from 101 upward. That is usually wrong. Sequencing by
physical relationship — a stack, a wing, a corridor, a shared utility run — means that when
something is confirmed, the units most likely to be related are searched within the same
hour rather than two days later. It also makes the resulting map easier to read, because
adjacency in the document matches adjacency in the building.

For properties large enough to span multiple days, three things decide the split. Which
units share assemblies, so related spaces stay together. Where the access risk is
concentrated, so the hardest block is not left until everyone is tired. And how the notices
were served, because residents told to expect a visit on Tuesday should not be knocked on
Wednesday.

There is a working constraint people rarely think about until they see it. A detection dog
works in short productive stretches, not continuously, and performance degrades as the day
goes on. We schedule to that limit rather than to a daily unit target, because a team pushed
past its useful window produces precisely the unreliable result the published field research
warns about. If a property genuinely needs two days, we would rather say so while quoting
than discover it at four in the afternoon with a third of the building left.

## How does the cost compare with handling it reactively?

Usually favourably, and the comparison people make is the wrong one.

The tempting comparison is the sweep quote against the cost of inspecting the one unit that
complained. On that basis a sweep always looks expensive, and it is the comparison most
managers make because it is the one sitting in front of them.

The comparison that matters is the sweep against the full cost of the reactive path: a
sequence of individual inspections spread over months, remediation in units that were
treated one at a time while the reservoir kept reseeding them, vacancy loss on units held out
of service while each episode is worked through, and the cost of a dispute that a
building-wide record would have closed. Puget Sound apartment vacancy was around 5.6 per
cent in the third quarter of 2025, with King County average rent at $2,126 according to the
UW Runstad Center, so a unit held out of service has a daily number attached to it.

None of that guarantees a sweep is cheaper for your building. It depends on how many units
are involved and how long the reactive path would have run. What it does mean is that a
sweep should be evaluated against the twelve-month cost of the alternative rather than
against a single inspection invoice, and that framing changes the answer more often than not.

## What are the honest limits?

Four, stated plainly.

**It is a snapshot.** A sweep describes the building on the days it was run. It does not
describe next month. For properties of any size the useful pattern is a cadence rather than
a one-off, which is what turns a set of incidents into a baseline you can reason from.

**It does not prove absence.** A written all-clear says what was searched and that nothing
was found on that date. It does not certify the building is free of bed bugs, and no
screening method can.

**Detection varies between teams.** The published field research shows a wide spread in
detection between certified canine teams working real infested apartments, which is why we
publish the handler, the dog, the certifying body and the dates rather than a percentage. The
numbers and the citations are at [detection accuracy](/detection-accuracy/).

**Clutter beats us sometimes.** A unit that cannot be searched properly is recorded as
inconclusive. That is an unsatisfying line in a report and it is what keeps the rest of the
document trustworthy.

coverage of a building nobody fully searched.
