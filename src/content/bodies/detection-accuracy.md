## Why publish the unflattering number at all?

Because you can find it in ten minutes, and because a company that hides it has told you
something about how it will behave when your report is inconvenient.

The field study below is not obscure. It is peer-reviewed, published in the same journal as
the study everybody quotes, and freely findable. Any property manager who does thirty
minutes of reading before hiring will come across it. The choice for a detection company is
not whether the client learns about the 44 percent figure. It is whether they learn about it
from you, in context, or from somebody else, in the middle of a dispute.

We would rather it be the first one. It also happens to be the honest way round.

## What exactly did the laboratory study measure?

Planted hides, under controlled conditions, in an environment built for the test.

Pfiester, Koehler and Pereira published in the *Journal of Economic Entomology* in 2008.
Trained teams were tested against known quantities of bed bugs placed in known conditions.
Detection was very high and false alerting was very low. The study is real, the
methodology is sound, and it is the origin of the figure of roughly 98 percent that
saturates this industry's marketing.

What it is not is a description of your building. A controlled environment has no clutter,
no competing odours from cooking or pets or cleaning products, no occupied furniture, no
residual scent from a past infestation that was treated two years ago, no temperature
gradients from a radiator under a window, and no resident standing in the doorway asking
what the dog is doing. Every one of those things is present in a real apartment and every
one of them affects a search.

Quoting the laboratory figure to a property manager is not lying. It is answering a
question nobody asked.

## What did the field study find?

Substantially less, and with a spread that is more informative than the average.

Cooper, Wang and Singh published in the same journal in 2014. They tested teams in
naturally infested apartments: real furniture, real clutter, real occupancy, real
infestations that nobody had planted. They reported a **mean detection rate of 44 percent,
ranging from 10 percent to 100 percent**, and a **mean false-positive rate of 15 percent,
ranging from 0 percent to 57 percent**. Their conclusion was that canine detection
capability under natural conditions may be more limited than controlled studies suggest.

Sit with those ranges for a moment, because the averages are the least interesting part.

One team in that study found essentially everything. Another found one target in ten. One
team produced no false alerts at all. Another produced false alerts on more than half of
its calls. These were all certified working teams, tested under the same protocol, in the
same category of building.

## Why is the range the actual finding?

Because a spread that wide means the variable being measured is not the species.

If canine detection were a fixed capability — a specification, like the resolution of a
camera — the results would cluster. They do not cluster. They spread across almost the
entire possible scale. That pattern tells you the outcome is dominated by something that
differs from team to team rather than by something inherent to dogs.

What differs between teams is training maintenance, blind-trial discipline, handler skill
at reading a subtle change in behaviour, search pattern, pace, how long the dog has already
worked that day, and whether the handler is willing to record an alert that turns out to be
wrong. Those are all human and organisational variables, not canine ones.

This reframes the buying decision completely. The question is not "are detection dogs
accurate," which has no useful answer. The question is "how do I tell a top-of-range team
from a bottom-of-range one," which has several.

## How do you tell a good team from a bad one?

Ask questions whose answers can be checked, and notice which ones produce discomfort.

**Ask who specifically is coming.** Which handler, which dog. A company that will not name
the working team before the visit is asking you to buy a specification it will fill later.

**Ask when the team last passed a blind test, and who proctored it.** A certification date
with no maintenance regime behind it describes a day in the past. Ask what the training
cadence is between certifications.

**Ask what happens when the dog alerts and nothing is found.** The answer you want is that
it is recorded as an unconfirmed alert. If the answer is that this does not happen, the
company is either not looking or not telling you.

**Ask for a sample report with the identifying details removed.** Look for whether
unconfirmed alerts appear in it at all. A report format with no category for them is a
format designed to hide them.

**Ask what the dog is certified to detect.** Live bed bugs and viable eggs are the useful
targets. A team that alerts on dead insects, cast skins and old faecal staining will
produce alerts in every unit that has ever had a problem, which is worse than useless in a
building with any history.

## What does a real double-blind test involve?

More than the word "certified" on its own conveys, which is why the standard matters more
than the certificate.

Under the Working Dog Detection Organization's bed bug canine detection test, double blind
means neither the team, the proctor, nor anyone else present knows how many targets have
been placed or where they are. Hide counts are randomised by dice roll, so even the pattern
of the test cannot be learned. At least one search area in each phase is blank, containing
nothing at all, which is the only way to measure false alerting honestly. An alert more
than three feet from the target is scored as false rather than being generously credited.
Basic odour recognition requires zero false alerts and zero misses to pass.

Compare that to a test where the proctor knows where the hides are. Dogs read handlers, and
handlers read proctors, without anyone intending it. A test that is not blind measures the
team's ability to detect a human being's expectations, which is a genuine canine skill and
completely irrelevant to your building.

When certification details for our own team are issued, they will be published here and on
the team page with the body, the standard and the dates. Until a field is filled with
something you can check, nothing is claimed in its place. A credential asserted without a
verifiable date is not a credential.

## How does visual confirmation change the arithmetic?

It converts the false-positive problem from a risk you carry into a line in a document.

The field study's false-positive range — 0 to 57 percent — is the number that should worry
a property manager more than the detection rate. A missed infestation is a problem you
still have. A false positive is a problem you never had, that you may now pay to treat.

Every alert we record is visually confirmed by the handler before it becomes a finding. The
result is written down in one of three states, and they are kept distinct because they mean
genuinely different things:

- **Confirmed.** Live insects, cast skins, eggs or faecal staining were seen and
  photographed at the alert location.
- **Not confirmed.** The dog alerted and nothing was visually found. Recorded as an alert,
  never upgraded to a finding.
- **Inconclusive.** Access, clutter or conditions prevented a proper look. Recorded as
  inconclusive, never recorded as clear.

That third category is the one that separates a useful document from a tidy one. A unit
that could not be properly searched is not a unit that is clear, and writing it down as
clear would be the single most damaging thing we could do to a client.

Nothing here eliminates the underlying variability. It makes it visible and auditable,
which is the most any inspection provider can honestly offer.

## When is a dog the wrong tool?

More often than the industry admits, and knowing when is part of the service.

**When you need proof of absence.** No screening method proves a negative. A written
all-clear says what was searched and found nothing on that date. It does not say the
building is free of bed bugs, and any document that claims to is overreaching.

**When the search area is one room.** If a single unit can be inspected visually in the
same time, the dog adds cost rather than coverage. Canine screening earns its place on
volume — forty units, a floor of a hotel, an auditorium of upholstered seating — where
visual inspection does not scale at all.

**When the building has extensive treatment history and the dog is not trained
discriminately.** In a building with a long history, residual scent sources are everywhere,
and a team that cannot discriminate will light up in units that have no live problem.

**When conditions are hostile to a search.** Extreme clutter, active cooking odours, very
high temperatures and a dog at the end of a long day all degrade results. We would rather
reschedule than produce a document we do not believe.

## Why do we not publish an accuracy figure of our own?

Because we have not run the study that would justify one, and a number without a study
behind it is just a nicer-sounding assertion.

It would be straightforward to write "our team detects at 95 percent" on this page. Nobody
would check it. There is no registry, no auditor and no requirement to substantiate it. That
is exactly why such figures are worth nothing, and why we would rather this page contain
somebody else's peer-reviewed research than our own marketing arithmetic.

What we can honestly tell you is the process: which standard the team is tested against, the
date it was last tested, who proctored it, what the maintenance training cadence is, and
that every alert is visually confirmed before it becomes a finding. Each of those is a fact
with a date attached that you can ask us to evidence. An accuracy percentage is not.

There is a related trap worth naming. A single team's performance on a single test is a
small sample, and small samples produce impressive-looking numbers in both directions. A
team that passed a test with no misses has demonstrated something real about that test. It
has not demonstrated a population-level detection rate, and presenting it as one would be
the same error the industry already makes with the laboratory study.

## How should you read any accuracy claim you are shown?

Ask four questions of the number, in this order.

**What were the conditions?** Planted hides in a prepared room and live infestations in an
occupied apartment are different measurements. If the claim does not say which, assume the
flattering one.

**Who was measured?** A published study of many teams tells you about the field. A vendor's
own result tells you about that vendor on that day, if it happened at all.

**Was the test blind?** If the proctor knew where the targets were, the result partly
measures the team's ability to read a human being. Dogs are extremely good at that, and it
is irrelevant to your building.

**What counted as a miss?** A generous scoring radius, no blank areas and an undisclosed
target count can turn a mediocre performance into a perfect score without anyone lying.

Apply those four questions to the figure most commonly quoted in this industry and it holds
up as a description of a laboratory and falls apart as a description of an apartment. Apply
them to the field study and it survives, which is why the uncomfortable number is the one we
build the business around.

## What we take from all of this

That the correct response to an uncomfortable statistic is to build the business around it
rather than around the comfortable one.

The 10-to-100 range is why we publish the handler and the dog by name instead of a
percentage. The false-positive range is why every alert is visually confirmed and why
unconfirmed alerts stay in the report. The gap between laboratory and field is why we do
not put a bare accuracy figure on any other page of this site, and why the compliance check
that enforces that rule fails our own build if we break it.
