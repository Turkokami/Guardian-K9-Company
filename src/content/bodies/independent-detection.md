## Why does the structure matter more than the intent?

Because incentives operate on the marginal call, and nobody has to be dishonest for the
outcome to shift.

Almost every canine bed bug inspection performed in this country is carried out by a
business that also sells bed bug treatment. Consider what that means at the level of a
single decision. A dog alerts. The handler looks. What is there is ambiguous: some dark
staining that might be faecal spotting or might be something else, in a unit with a history
nobody can reconstruct.

That call has to be made hundreds of times a year. If one answer produces a treatment sale
and the other produces a polite goodbye, the ambiguous calls do not need to be decided
dishonestly for the aggregate to bend. Human judgement under repeated financial pressure
bends by itself. This is not a claim about the character of anybody in the pest control
industry, most of whom are careful people doing skilled work. It is a claim about what
happens to a distribution of judgements when you attach a consequence to one tail of it.

The person reading the report has no way of telling which findings were marginal. That is
the actual problem. Not that the inspection is wrong, but that its reliability cannot be
assessed from the outside.

## What does structural independence look like in practice?

Four things, all of which are checkable, none of which is a promise about character.

**No treatment revenue.** Guardian applies no pesticides and sells no treatment of any kind.
There is no version of the visit that ends with us quoting remediation, because we are not
licensed to perform it and are not in that business.

**No referral consideration.** We take no fee, no commission and no revenue share from any
applicator for work that follows an inspection. If we confirm a problem and you ask who to
call, you get told to engage a licensed applicator of your own choosing, and the choosing is
genuinely yours. This is the part most worth asking other vendors about directly, because
referral arrangements are common and rarely volunteered.

**Vendor neutrality.** You can pair our inspection with any treatment provider you like,
including one you already have under contract. A large share of our commercial work is
exactly this: an independent verification layer sitting over an existing pest relationship
that the client wants to keep.

**Separated recording.** Every alert is visually confirmed before it becomes a finding, and
confirmed, not confirmed and inconclusive are recorded as three distinct states rather than
being collapsed into one. That is what turns our independence from an assertion into
something visible in the document.

## Who benefits most from this arrangement?

Anybody who has to justify a decision to a third party.

If you are a property manager arguing about who pays, a document produced by a company with
no stake in the answer is worth more than one produced by a company that profits from a
particular answer. Your tenant's advocate will make that point if you do not.

If you are a facilities director whose pest contractor has just told you a problem is
resolved, an independent verification is the only way to test that claim without simply
trusting the party being tested. An operator verifying its own remediation has an obvious
difficulty, and no amount of professionalism removes it.

If you are an HOA board or a public body, procurement rules and minutes tend to reward
demonstrable independence. Being able to record that the inspection was carried out by a
party with no interest in the outcome is a governance asset.

If you are a hotel or venue operator responding to a guest complaint, the same document that
protects you when the complaint is unfounded also directs the response efficiently when it
is not. A finding with no commercial direction behind it is more persuasive in both
directions.

## What are the honest costs of buying this way?

Three, and we would rather list them than have you discover them.

**The inspection line costs more.** A company that treats can price an inspection at
nothing, or close to it, because the inspection is a lead-generation expense recovered on
the treatment. We have no second transaction to recover it on, so the inspection carries its
own cost. If you compare our line item against a free inspection, we lose that comparison
every time. Whether we lose the overall comparison depends entirely on how much treatment
you were buying that you did not need.

**It is two relationships instead of one.** Some buyers genuinely prefer a single vendor
managing detection and remediation end to end, and there is a real convenience argument
there. What you trade for that convenience is the independence of the finding. That is a
legitimate trade for some organisations and a bad one for anybody who expects to be arguing
about causation.

**We will not tell you what to do next.** When you ask which treatment approach to use, we
will decline. Recommending pesticide products or methods to a user is separately licensed
activity in Washington under RCW 15.58.210, and Guardian does not hold that licence. Some
clients find that frustrating, and it is a real limitation of buying detection from a
detection-only business. The alternative is a vendor who advises you on a product it also
sells, which is the thing we are trying not to be.

## How do you tell an audit from a sales inspection?

Six questions, and the discomfort produced by each is as informative as the answer.

1. **Does the inspecting company profit if the answer is yes?** The single most clarifying
   question in this entire market. Ask it plainly.
2. **Do you take any fee or consideration for referrals?** A hesitation here is an answer.
3. **Is every alert visually confirmed before it is written down, or is the alert itself the
   finding?** If the alert is the finding, the report cannot distinguish between a detection
   and a false positive, and the published field research says false positives are common
   enough to matter.
4. **Does the report state what was *not* searched?** A document that omits its own gaps is
   presenting incomplete coverage as complete coverage.
5. **Will you name the handler and the dog before the visit?** If the working team is
   assigned later, you are buying a category rather than a capability.
6. **Can I see a sample report with the details removed?** Look specifically for whether
   there is a category for unconfirmed alerts. A format with no such category is a format
   designed not to have any.

An audit answers all six comfortably. A sales inspection typically answers one or two and
changes the subject on the rest.

## What does the referral conversation actually sound like?

Shorter and less helpful than clients expect, and deliberately so.

When we confirm a problem, the exchange that follows is roughly this. You ask what you
should do about it. We tell you that you will need a licensed applicator, that the report
maps exactly where the confirmed findings are, and that whoever you engage should be given
that map because it will make their scoping more accurate and their quote more honest. You
ask who we would use. We tell you we cannot answer that.

That is not evasiveness or false modesty. Under **RCW 15.58.210**, a person who offers or
supplies technical advice or makes recommendations to the user of a pesticide, other than
products labelled and intended for home and garden use only, is acting as a pest control
consultant, and that requires a licence Guardian does not hold. The boundary is not a
stylistic preference. It is a statute with a licensing requirement attached, and the moment
we start ranking products or methods for you we are on the wrong side of it.

What we can do is neutral and still useful. We can describe, in general terms, the
categories of approach the industry uses, so that you are not walking into a quote
conversation unarmed. We can tell you what questions to ask about scope, preparation
requirements and follow-up verification. We can tell you what our own report does and does
not establish. And we can come back afterwards and check the result independently, which is
the part where our lack of a stake becomes most obviously valuable.

Clients occasionally find this frustrating, and it is a genuine limitation of buying from a
detection-only business rather than a full-service one. The alternative is advice from a
company that profits from the advice.

## What happens when we disagree with another report?

We document the disagreement and let you decide, rather than adjudicating it.

Second-opinion work is one of the more common reasons people call. Typically a treatment
company has inspected, found something, and quoted; or has treated, declared success, and
the complaints have continued. In either case somebody is being asked to accept a
conclusion from a party with an interest in it.

Our job in that situation is narrow. We search independently and record what we find,
including where we find nothing. We do not annotate the other company's report, characterise
its motives, or tell you it was wrong. If our findings differ from theirs, the document says
what we searched, on what date, and what the confirmation results were. Two dated records
from two parties, one of which has no financial stake, is a much stronger position for you
than either record alone.

Sometimes our findings support the other report entirely, and we say so as plainly as we
would say the opposite. A second opinion that always disagrees with the first is not an
independent check, it is a different sales pitch. If we only ever confirmed that other
companies were wrong, you should trust us exactly as little as you trust a treatment company
that always finds something.

There is also a case people do not expect: we sometimes find that the original inspection
was more thorough than ours could be, usually because access has changed or because a unit
has been cleaned or disturbed since. We record that too, as an inconclusive result rather
than a clean one.

## What independence does not buy you

It is not a claim about accuracy, and conflating the two would be its own kind of dishonesty.

Independence removes a systematic bias from the reporting of what was found. It does nothing
whatsoever about the underlying variability of canine detection itself, which the published
field research shows is substantial and driven mainly by differences between working teams.
An independent team can still be a poor team. A conflicted team can be excellent.

So independence and competence are two separate questions, and you should ask both. We
address the second one on the [detection accuracy](/detection-accuracy/) page, in more
detail and with less flattery than is customary, because a company that leans on its
independence to avoid discussing its capability is doing a version of the same trick.

Independence also does not make us neutral about our own interests. We would like the work.
What it means is narrower and more useful than neutrality: on the specific question of what
was found in your building, our revenue does not move with the answer.

## Why does procurement care about this?

Because an independent finding is easier to defend than a self-interested one, and
defensibility is what procurement is buying.

Public bodies, housing authorities, institutional facilities departments and HOA boards all
operate under some obligation to show that a decision was reasonable. The mechanism differs
— a minuted board decision, a procurement file, an audit trail — but the underlying test is
similar. Somebody may later ask why you spent the money, or why you did not.

A report from a party with no financial interest in the answer survives that question better
than one from a party with an obvious interest. It is not that the conflicted report is
wrong. It is that defending it requires an argument, and the independent one does not.

The same logic applies on the insurance and liability side. Where a claim or a dispute turns
on what a property owner knew and when, a dated third-party document is a materially
different piece of evidence from an internal note or a vendor's assurance. That is also why
the all-clear matters as much as the finding: proving you checked and found nothing is
frequently the more valuable record.

For vendor onboarding, the practical questions are usually about insurance certificates,
scope of work and who signs the report. Ours is signed by the handler who performed the
inspection rather than issued in a company name, which is a small thing that matters
enormously when somebody is asked to stand behind it later.

## Where this shows up in the work

In three documents, mainly.

The [inspection report](/services/k9-bed-bug-detection/) records confirmed and unconfirmed
alerts separately and states its own coverage limits. The
[post-treatment verification](/services/bed-bug-verification-inspection/) is an independent
check on somebody else's work, which is the clearest case where a conflicted inspection is
worth very little. And the written all-clear, the least glamorous of the three, is the one
that matters most eighteen months later, because it is a dated statement from a party with
no reason to have written it unless it was true.

The standard those documents are held to is set out at
[what makes a report defensible](/compliance/what-a-defensible-report-contains/). If you
have an existing report from another provider and want a second opinion on it, that is a
routine request and one of the more common reasons people call.

One last point, because it is the fairest test of whether any of this is real. Independence
is only worth something if it survives the occasion when it costs us money. That occasion is
the visit where we search a building thoroughly, find nothing at all, and invoice for a day's
work that produced no follow-up and no further business. Those visits are the product working
correctly rather than a disappointing outcome, and a company that quietly resents them will
eventually stop having them.
