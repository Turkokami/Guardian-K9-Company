## What is this document actually for?

Answering a question somebody asks much later, when nobody remembers the visit.

An inspection report feels, on the day, like a receipt. It is not. Its real function begins
months afterwards, when a tenant's advocate asks what the landlord knew and when, or an
owner asks why money was spent, or an insurer asks what steps were taken, or a board asks
why a decision was made the way it was.

At that point nobody involved remembers the visit. The document is all there is, and it will
be read by somebody who was not present, has no reason to be generous, and is specifically
looking for the gap between what it says and what it implies.

That is the standard to write to. Not "does this record what we found," but "does this
survive a hostile reading by a stranger in two years."

## What has to be on it?

Seven elements. Most reports in this industry carry four.

**A date.** Obvious, routinely present, and the single most load-bearing item. An undated
finding establishes nothing about sequence, and sequence is what most disputes turn on.

**A name and a signature.** The person who performed the inspection, not just the company
that invoiced for it. Canine detection results vary enormously between working teams, so
"a certified team attended" is materially less informative than knowing which one. A report
that cannot be traced to an individual is a report nobody has to stand behind.

**A defined scope.** Which building, which units, which rooms, which areas within them. A
report that says "the property was inspected" invites the reader to assume everything was
covered, which is almost never true.

**An alert map.** Every location the dog indicated on, recorded against the unit and the
position within it. Not a count. A location. "Three alerts in the building" is not usable;
"alerts at the headboard seam in 204, the sofa frame in 206, and the skirting junction in
310" is.

**A confirmation result against every alert.** Three states, kept distinct, covered in the
next section.

**A statement of what was not searched.** The element most commonly missing and the one that
does the most damage by its absence.

**Photographs of confirmed findings**, where confirmation was physically possible.

## Why do the three confirmation states matter so much?

Because collapsing them into one destroys the report's ability to be checked.

**Confirmed** means the handler examined the alert location and saw physical evidence: live
insects, cast skins, eggs or faecal staining, photographed where possible. This is a finding.

**Not confirmed** means the dog alerted and nothing was visually found. It is recorded as an
alert, and it is never quietly promoted into a finding. It might be residual odour from a
previous infestation, a scent source in an adjoining void, or the dog being wrong.

**Inconclusive** means access, clutter or conditions prevented a proper look. It is never
recorded as clear, because a unit that could not be searched is not a unit that is clean.

The temptation to remove the middle category is strong, and giving in to it is the single
most common way a report becomes untrustworthy. A document in which every alert was
confirmed does not read as impressive to anyone who understands the published field data on
canine false positives. It reads as a document that has been tidied.

The inverse failure is treating every alert as a finding, which inflates the apparent
problem and, when the inspecting company also sells treatment, inflates the invoice. Keeping
the states separate is what makes the report legible to a third party rather than something
they simply have to trust.

## Why does "what was not searched" carry so much weight?

Because a coverage gap that is not disclosed becomes a coverage claim that is false.

Consider a fifty-unit sweep where six units could not be entered. If the report lists
forty-four units with their results and says nothing else, a reader in two years will
reasonably conclude the building was screened. It was not. Eighty-eight per cent of it was.

The gap matters most in exactly the scenario where the report gets read: something surfaces
later in one of the six. At that point the omission is not a formatting choice, it is the
centre of the dispute, and the party that produced the document is in a much worse position
than if it had simply written the six unit numbers down.

Common reasons for a gap, all of which should be recorded plainly: no answer at the door,
access refused, a pet that could not be secured, clutter preventing a search, a room locked
and no key available, or conditions that made a reliable search impossible on the day.
None of these reflect badly on anybody. Concealing them does.

This is also why the entry-notice question is a documentation question rather than an
administrative one. Notices served properly under **RCW 59.18.150** are what produce the
open doors that make a sweep complete. Notices served late produce a report full of gaps.
The detail is at
[entry notice for unit inspections](/compliance/entry-notice-for-unit-inspections/).

## How does the report interact with the statute?

It is how a landlord demonstrates the thing the statute requires but does not define.

**RCW 59.18.060(4)** obliges a landlord to provide a *reasonable programme* for pest control
and, other than in a single-family residence, to control infestation during the tenancy
except where the tenant caused it. The statute does not say what reasonable looks like. It
does not specify a method, a frequency or a standard.

That vagueness is usually read as a weakness. It is better understood as the reason
documentation matters. Where a standard is undefined, what you have instead is a record of
what you actually did, and a dated series of inspections with written results is a far
stronger account of reasonableness than an assertion after the fact.

The tenant-causation carve-out works the same way. **RCW 59.18.130** is the route to tenant
liability where the tenant caused the infestation, and causation is an evidentiary question.
A report establishes distribution and timing across units and dates. It does not establish
origin, and it should never be presented as though it does.

The full statutory picture is at
[who is responsible under Washington landlord-tenant law](/compliance/washington-landlord-tenant-bed-bugs/).
Where a municipal layer applies, as it does in Seattle, the documentation expectations rise
accordingly; that is covered at
[Seattle rental bed bug rules](/compliance/seattle-rental-bed-bug-rules/).

## Does it matter who produced it?

To the reader, considerably.

A report produced by a company that also sells the remediation carries an incentive the
reader has to account for. That does not make it wrong. It makes its reliability
unassessable from outside, because the marginal judgement calls, the ones where the evidence
was ambiguous, are exactly the calls a financial interest acts on, and they are invisible in
the finished document.

An independent report removes that variable. It does not remove the underlying variability
of canine detection itself, which is a separate question and a real one, addressed at
[detection accuracy](/detection-accuracy/). Independence and competence are two different
things and you should ask about both.

For institutional buyers, public bodies and HOA boards this tends to be decisive, because
their obligation is usually to show a decision was reasonable, and a finding from a party
with no stake in the answer is simply easier to defend than one that needs explaining first.
More on that at [why independence is the product](/independent-detection/).

## What are the common failures?

Six, and none of them involves anybody lying.

**No date on the finding itself.** A report dated at the top, with findings that could have
been recorded at any point during a multi-day engagement, is weaker than it looks. Each
unit's result should carry its own date where the work spanned more than a day.

**Alert counts instead of alert locations.** "Two alerts in unit 214" is an administrative
note. "Alerts at the box spring seam and the base of the adjacent nightstand in unit 214" is
a document somebody can act on and check.

**Photographs without context.** A close-up of staining that does not identify which unit,
which surface and which alert it corresponds to cannot be tied to anything later.

**Conclusions the method does not support.** "The infestation originated in unit 214" is not
a finding a detection inspection can produce. Distribution and timing are. Origin is not, and
a report asserting it undermines the parts that were defensible.

**Recommendations from an unlicensed party.** In Washington, offering technical advice or
recommendations to a pesticide user is licensed activity under RCW 15.58.210. A detection
report that names products or ranks methods is doing something its author may well not be
licensed to do, which is a problem for its author and a weakness in the document.

**Silent scope reduction.** The building had sixty units; the report covers fifty-two; the
report does not mention sixty. This is the most damaging one and the most common.

## How should it be stored, and who can get it back?

Longer than feels sensible, and by somebody who is still reachable.

The request for a report almost never arrives while it is still fresh. It arrives when a
tenancy ends badly, when a property changes hands, when an insurer asks, or when an attorney
sends a letter. By then the manager who commissioned it may have moved on, the email may sit
in a deactivated mailbox, and the only surviving copy may be a PDF attachment nobody can
find.

Two practical habits fix most of this. Keep reports in the property file rather than in
personal correspondence, so they survive staff turnover. And confirm with your provider that
they retain their own copy and will reissue it. We do, because we have had enough of those
requests to know they come.

For portfolios, consistency of format matters as much as retention. Twelve buildings with
twelve differently structured documents from four vendors is a filing cabinet. Twelve
buildings with one format on one cadence is a baseline, and a baseline is what lets you say
what changed and when.

## Does a goose program produce the same kind of record?

A different document with the same underlying logic.

Grounds work does not generate alert maps. What it generates is a visit log: the date, which
areas were worked, what was observed, and what changed since the previous visit. For an HOA
board or a public grounds manager that log is frequently the actual deliverable rather than
a by-product, because the governance question is whether the body responded to a nuisance
proportionately and on advice.

The same disciplines apply. It is dated. It says what was done rather than what was
achieved. It does not claim outcomes the method cannot deliver, which in that context means
it never promises that the geese are permanently gone. And it records the standing operating
limits — no contact, no injury, no nests or eggs disturbed, and the published pause during
the flightless molt — so that the record itself demonstrates the work stayed inside them.

That is set out at [K9 goose deterrent](/services/k9-goose-deterrent/) and
[Canada goose law in Washington](/compliance/canada-goose-law-washington/).

## What does the report deliberately not do?

Four things, and a report that does them anyway is weaker for it rather than stronger.

**It does not establish origin.** No inspection method can determine who introduced bed bugs
into a building. What a report establishes is distribution and timing: which spaces were
searched, on which dates, what was confirmed and what was clear. That is the evidence a
causation argument is built from. The argument itself involves facts about occupancy,
movement and history that no inspector observes.

**It does not certify absence.** A written all-clear states what was searched on a given
date and that nothing was found. It does not say the building is free of bed bugs, and it
cannot, because no screening method proves a negative. The published field research on
canine detection makes that unambiguous. Any document promising a clean bill of health is
claiming more than the method supports, and the promise is precisely the part that fails
under scrutiny.

**It does not recommend a remedy.** Naming products, ranking methods or advising on
application is licensed activity in Washington under RCW 15.58.210. A detection report that
does it is either produced by a licensed consultant, in which case the commercial
relationship is worth examining, or by somebody outside their licence. Our reports describe
findings and stop.

**It does not grade another provider's work.** Where we are engaged for a second opinion, we
inspect independently and produce our own dated record. We do not annotate somebody else's
document or characterise their motives. Two independent records, one from a party with no
financial stake, is a stronger position for the client than our opinion of a competitor.

There is a reason to be explicit about all four. A document that overreaches in one place
invites a reader to discount it everywhere, and the elements that were genuinely solid get
dragged down with the one that was not. A report that states its own limits is not a weaker
document. It is the only kind that holds up when somebody is actively looking for the gap.

## What should you ask for before you hire anyone?

Four requests, and the reaction to each is informative.

**A sample report with identifying details removed.** Look for a category for unconfirmed
alerts and a section for areas not searched. If neither exists in the template, neither will
exist in yours.

**The name of the handler who will attend.** Assigned in advance, not on the day.

**Their retention policy.** How long the file is kept and whether it can be reissued. Ours
is retained so it can be, because the request nearly always arrives long after the visit.

**Whether they profit if the answer is yes.** The most clarifying question available in this
market, and worth asking directly.

A provider comfortable with all four is describing an audit. A provider uncomfortable with
two of them is describing a sales inspection, which may still be useful, but should not be
relied on as evidence in a dispute where the other side gets to ask the same questions.

## What is the shortest version of all this?

A defensible report is one whose author expected it to be read by somebody hostile.

Everything above follows from that single idea. The date is there because sequence gets
disputed. The signature is there because somebody has to stand behind it. The scope
statement is there because coverage gets overstated by omission. The three confirmation
states are there because collapsing them hides the judgement calls. The list of what was not
searched is there because that is the first thing an opposing reader will look for and the
last thing a careless report contains.

None of it is expensive to do. It is a format decision, made once, and then followed even on
the visits where nothing is found and nobody expects the document to matter. Those are
precisely the visits whose paperwork turns out to matter most, because a clean result on a
recorded date is what establishes a timeline.

If you are commissioning this kind of work, the test is simple enough to apply before you
sign anything: ask to see the template, and check whether it has somewhere to write down the
awkward answers. A format with nowhere to record an unconfirmed alert or an unsearched unit
is a format that will never produce one.
