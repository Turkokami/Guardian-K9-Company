## What makes Anacortes different from the rest of Skagit?

Three economies on one island, none of which behaves like the valley floor.

Most of Skagit is agricultural land, the I-5 corridor and the county seat. Anacortes sits out
on Fidalgo Island and runs on heavy industry, a state ferry terminal and the marine trades.
The result is a town whose accommodation and workforce patterns have almost nothing in common
with [Mount Vernon](/service-area/mount-vernon/) or [Burlington](/service-area/burlington/)
twenty minutes inland.

**March Point** carries two refineries: the HF Sinclair Puget Sound Refinery, running at
around 149,000 barrels per day and one of the largest employers in Skagit County, and the
Marathon Anacortes Refinery at around 120,000 barrels per day. **The Washington State Ferries
terminal** is the mainland gateway to the San Juan Islands, which brings seasonal passenger
volume through the town. And **the marine trades** — boatyards, moorage, liveaboards —
produce inspection settings that are not buildings at all.

Each of those creates a different screening question, and a company that treats Anacortes as
a generic small city will answer all three wrong.

## Why do refinery turnarounds set the calendar?

Because they concentrate high-mobility occupancy into a known window, months in advance.

A scheduled maintenance turnaround brings a large contractor workforce into the area for a
defined period. Those crews need accommodation, and they arrive having travelled from other
job sites, frequently having stayed in comparable accommodation at the last one. That is
high-mobility occupancy, which is the pattern that produces most of the introduction
opportunity in this industry.

This is not a comment about contractors. It is a description of how often a bed changes hands
and how far the previous occupant travelled to reach it, which is the only variable that
actually matters.

What makes it unusually workable is that the risk is **scheduled**. Unlike a complaint, which
arrives without warning, a turnaround is planned well ahead. That converts screening from a
reactive expense into a calendar decision. The weeks immediately after a turnaround are the
single highest-value screening window in the local year, and the weeks before are the second,
because that is when a room can still be held if something is found.

Operators who screen against the turnaround calendar find things while they are small.
Operators who wait for a guest complaint find them after the review is written. The lodging
setting is at [hotels and lodging](/commercial/hotels-and-lodging/), and the same pattern
around Cherry Point is described on the [Ferndale](/service-area/ferndale/) page.

## What does island geography actually change?

Travel time, ferry dependency, and what can honestly be promised.

Fidalgo Island is reached by road, so Anacortes itself is a normal drive with a little more
time than the corridor towns. That is a scheduling factor rather than an obstacle, and it is
accounted for openly in a quote rather than added afterwards.

Work beyond Fidalgo is a different matter. Anything depending on a sailing carries a hard
constraint that no amount of goodwill removes: if the boat is full or the timetable changes,
the appointment changes with it. So island work is scheduled with margin. We would rather
book a date a week out that holds than offer one in two days and move it.

There is a corollary worth knowing if you hold several properties. Grouping them into a
single trip is both cheaper and more reliable than booking them separately, because the fixed
cost of getting there is spread and there is one timetable to work around instead of several.

Seasonal visitor volume through the ferry terminal follows the same logic as any tourism
market: screen on the shoulders of the season rather than through it, because during the peak
no room can be taken out of service and that is precisely when you least want an answer.

## What about boats, boatyards and liveaboards?

They are genuine inspection settings and they are not apartments.

A vessel is a confined space with soft furnishings, upholstered berths, bedding, storage
lockers and a great deal of joinery, in a hull that cannot be opened up the way a wall can.
Liveaboard moorage adds a residential dimension without the building. Boatyards and marine
service businesses have their own access and safety arrangements.

None of that makes the work impossible. It makes it different to scope, and the honest
position is that berth access, who is authorised to grant it, and what can physically be
searched all differ from a building. A quote written on residential assumptions would be
wrong, so we ask about the setting before we price it.

The recording discipline is the same everywhere: anything we could not properly search is
recorded as inconclusive rather than clear. In a confined space with limited access, that
word appears more often than it does in an apartment, and pretending otherwise would make the
document worth less rather than more.

## What governs a rental here?

State law, with no municipal layer.

**RCW 59.18.060(4)** requires a landlord to provide a reasonable pest control programme at
the initiation of a tenancy and, other than in a single-family residence, to control
infestation during the tenancy except where the tenant caused it. **RCW 59.18.130** is the
route to tenant cost liability where causation is established. **RCW 59.18.150** requires at
least two days written notice to enter and inspect a tenanted unit.

Anacortes adds nothing naming bed bugs specifically, and neither does the rest of Skagit.
Seattle is the exception statewide, where bed bugs are named explicitly and penalties run
from $150 to $500 per day.

The mixed-portfolio caution applies here more than almost anywhere in our service area,
because a single owner may hold a long-term rental, a short-stay unit and a moorage
arrangement, and those sit under three different frameworks. Landlord-tenant law governs the
first, accommodation obligations the second, and the third is its own question. One internal
policy will not fit all three. The tenancy side is at
[who is responsible under Washington landlord-tenant law](/compliance/washington-landlord-tenant-bed-bugs/).

## Does the older townsite stock change the search?

It changes how long a proper search takes, which is a cost question rather than a risk one.

Anacortes has a substantial older townsite housing stock alongside marine-industrial
buildings and newer waterfront development. Older wood-frame construction offers far more
harbourage in trim, skirting voids and wall assemblies than sealed modern building, so there
is more to search and the search is slower. A scoping assumption carried over from newer
stock will be wrong.

Newer construction is not lower risk and it would be misleading to imply it. Bed bugs arrive
with people and belongings, not with buildings. What newer construction changes is where they
can go once they arrive and how quickly a room can be ruled out.

Clutter matters more than either, and more than floor area. A heavily occupied small unit can
take longer than a large sparse one. Where clutter or access prevents a proper search, the
space is recorded as inconclusive, which is the least satisfying line in any report and the
one that keeps the rest of it trustworthy.

## Are the geese here worth a programme?

On managed waterfront turf and institutional grounds, frequently.

Resident Canada geese settle on mown grass with open sightlines and water nearby, which
describes a good deal of the maintained shoreline, park turf and campus-style ground in and
around the town. The consequence is fouled walkways and grass that stops being usable.

Washington Department of Fish and Wildlife's Living with Wildlife guidance names
handler-directed dogs as the method of choice for large open areas such as parks, playing
fields and corporate grounds. The same guidance is explicit that dogs can be inappropriate in
continuously used parks, near roadways and on large water bodies, and a waterfront town has
plenty of ground in the second category. We say so when a site is the wrong shape for the
method.

What we do is handler-directed hazing of resident Canada geese: no contact, no injury, no
nests or eggs disturbed. Canada geese are federally protected under the Migratory Bird Treaty
Act, and the federal definition of take at 50 CFR 10.12 includes pursuit. Registration under
the Resident Canada Goose Nest and Egg Depredation Order at 50 CFR 21.162 belongs to the
landowner, association or local government rather than to a vendor, so nest and egg work is
not ours to offer. Hazing pauses during the flightless molt as published operating policy.

Public parkland requires the land manager to engage us directly. A neighbouring property
owner cannot authorise work on ground they do not control, however genuinely the flock is
their problem too. And as everywhere in this valley, the enormous winter flocks the region is
known for are **snow geese** under a different management framework, which we do not work.
The legal detail is at
[Canada goose law in Washington](/compliance/canada-goose-law-washington/).

## Who calls us here?

Four kinds of buyer, wanting three different things.

**Accommodation operators**, screening around the turnaround calendar and the ferry season.
They need speed and discretion, and for public-facing property we work without marked
vehicles or branded clothing.

**Industrial and contractor housing managers**, dealing with crews who arrive together and
leave together. The screening question is about the whole facility at a point in time rather
than about one unit.

**Residential landlords and small property managers**, usually after a tenant report. What
they need is a document that settles responsibility before an argument hardens, and a
screening pattern that covers neighbouring units rather than only the one that complained.

**Marine businesses and vessel owners**, who mostly want to know whether this is even a thing
that can be done in their setting. It is, with the caveats above stated plainly.

What all four are buying is the same underlying thing: a dated record from a party with no
financial interest in the answer. That is why the document format does not change between
them.

## How should a turnaround screening programme be structured?

Around the published schedule, in three passes, none of which is expensive on its own.

**Before.** Screen the accommodation that will house the crews while rooms can still be held.
Anything found at this point is cheap to deal with, because the property is not yet full and
the alternative is finding it while every bed is committed.

**During, selectively.** Not the whole property, which is impossible when it is full, but a
rotating handful of rooms if the turnaround runs long. This is the pass most operators skip
and the one that catches a problem while it is still confined to a few rooms.

**After.** The most valuable of the three. A large transient population has just cycled
through, the property is emptying, and rooms can be taken out of service again. This is the
pass that stops one turnaround's problem becoming the baseline condition for the next.

The reason this works is that the dates are published well in advance, which is unusual in
this field. Almost everything else we screen against is either continuous or reactive. A
turnaround is a known event with a known start and end, so the screening can be booked
alongside it rather than negotiated afterwards.

For contractor housing specifically, the question is usually about a whole facility at a
point in time rather than about individual units, which changes the scope and generally
improves the price per bed.

## One last thing about island work

Reliability matters more here than speed, and the two are usually in tension.

On the mainland a missed appointment costs an afternoon. On an island it can cost the whole
trip, because there is no rescheduling to later the same day and the next available slot may
be a week out. That asymmetry is why we quote island work with margin built in rather than
quoting the fastest date that is theoretically possible.

It also changes what a client should ask for. The useful question is not how soon we can come
but which date we will actually hold, and any vendor who answers the first question eagerly
without addressing the second has not thought about the geography. We would rather lose a job
to somebody promising Tuesday than take it and move it to the following Thursday.

The same principle governs how we handle access. On a mainland property, a missing key means
an hour's delay. Out here it can mean the property does not get searched at all on that trip,
so the access plan gets confirmed before the date rather than on the morning.
