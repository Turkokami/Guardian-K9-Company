## Why does this page exist at all?

Because a town this size deserves an honest answer rather than a page pretending it is
Bellingham.

Concrete sits in the upper Skagit valley, a long way from the Interstate 5 corridor where
most of this county's population and nearly all of its commercial property sits. The
permanent rental market is very small. Most property decisions are made by individuals rather
than management companies. And the drive is real: getting a handler and a dog up the valley
and back is most of a day.

A lot of service-area pages for towns like this are written as though geography does not
exist, promising the same response time offered in a city forty miles away. We would rather
say plainly what the constraint is, because a date that holds is worth more than a date that
sounds good, and because you will find out either way.

So: yes, we cover Concrete. It is booked in advance with the travel accounted for in the
schedule and stated openly in the quote. It is not same-day work and we will not pretend
otherwise.

## How should upper valley work actually be booked?

Grouped, if at all possible.

The economics here are dominated by a fixed cost, which is the journey. That cost is the same
whether we search one cabin or six properties, which means the price per property falls
sharply the more there is to do on a single trip.

The practical consequence is that grouping is worth actively organising. If you own several
properties, book them together. If you know other owners nearby who have been thinking about
it, say so when you call and we will schedule the trip around all of you. This is genuinely
one of those situations where coordinating with neighbours saves everybody real money, and it
is the advice we would give whether or not we were the ones doing the work.

The same logic applies to timing. A trip planned a fortnight out can be built into a route
that makes sense. A trip requested for tomorrow cannot, and the price reflects that if we can
do it at all.

## What kind of property is up here?

Two kinds, with different rhythms.

**Permanent housing**, mostly older river-corridor stock held by individual owners. Early
construction offers more harbourage in trim, skirting voids and wall assemblies than modern
building does, so a search takes longer per unit than the same nominal space on the corridor.
That is a cost and scheduling factor rather than a statement about likelihood; bed bugs
arrive with people and belongings, not with buildings.

**Seasonal recreation lodging**, around Baker Lake, the Baker River valley and the State
Route 20 corridor into the North Cascades. These fill heavily during a short season and sit
largely empty outside it, which produces a profile that appears almost nowhere else in our
service area.

That second pattern is worth understanding properly. A property occupied intensively for a
few months and then unoccupied for the rest of the year can carry something introduced at the
end of one season straight through to the next, to be discovered all at once by people who
reasonably assume it is a current problem. Long vacancy does not resolve an infestation on
any timescale an owner should plan around, and the absence of complaints during a vacancy is
not evidence of anything at all, because there was nobody there to complain.

## When is the right time to screen a seasonal property?

Before the season opens. Not after a guest calls.

This is the clearest scheduling advice on this page and it applies to every cabin, holiday
let and short-stay property up the valley. Before the season, the unit is empty and fully
accessible, no entry notice arises, nothing is in the way, and if something is found there is
still time to arrange remediation before anybody has paid to stay there.

Screening in the middle of the season is the opposite of all of that. Every unit is committed,
taking one out of service costs the most it will cost all year, and the decision gets made
under pressure with a booking in progress.

An empty unit is also the cheapest and most complete search we do anywhere, which is why the
same argument turns up on the [turnover screening](/services/turnover-screening/) page. In an
unoccupied property almost nothing ends up recorded as inconclusive, so the result is better
data as well as a lower price.

## What governs a rental here?

State law, with nothing added locally.


Concrete adds no municipal provision naming bed bugs, and neither does the rest of Skagit.
Seattle is the statewide outlier, with bed bugs named explicitly and penalties running from
$150 to $500 per day.

Two things are worth pulling out for owners here specifically. The **single-family carve-out**
matters more than usual, because a large share of the rental property up the valley is houses
rather than apartments; it removes the ongoing control duty for that property type but not
the duty to provide a reasonable programme at the start of a tenancy. And **a holiday let is
not a tenancy**, so an owner with both a long-term rental and a seasonal cabin is working
under two different frameworks at once. The tenancy side is at
[who is responsible under Washington landlord-tenant law](/compliance/washington-landlord-tenant-bed-bugs/).

## When would we tell you not to bother?

More often here than anywhere else we work, and it is worth explaining why.

Canine screening earns its place on volume. Its advantage over a careful visual inspection is
that it covers many spaces quickly and samples odour from voids and assemblies nobody can
open. In a single room, a thorough visual inspection by somebody who knows what to look for
can answer the same question in a comparable time and for less money.

Up the valley, single-property enquiries are the norm rather than the exception. So the
honest answer to "should you hire us for one cabin" is frequently no, or at least not on its
own. It becomes worth it when there are several properties to do in one trip, when a visual
inspection has already been done and left the question open, when somebody needs a document
rather than an opinion, or when the property has a history that makes a proper search
worthwhile.

We would rather give that answer and lose the booking. A company that tells you to spend money
you did not need to spend gets one job from you. This is also the practical reason grouped
visits are the main way this works up here.

If you do need a document specifically, that is a different question from needing a search,
and it is usually the answer when there is a dispute, an insurer or a purchaser involved.
What such a document has to contain is at
[what makes a report defensible](/compliance/what-a-defensible-report-contains/).

## Does the river matter?

Through what follows a water event, as everywhere on this valley floor.

The upper valley has a long relationship with high water. The relevance to this work is not
the water but the aftermath: remediation brings replacement furnishings into buildings, often
second-hand or donated, and displaced households move through temporary accommodation. Those
are documented introduction pathways and they arrive on a schedule nobody plans.

The most severe recent example in our service area is a county north, where the November 2021
Nooksack flooding damaged the overwhelming majority of structures in Sumas and displaced
hundreds of people. That is written up on the [Sumas](/service-area/sumas/) and
[Everson](/service-area/everson/) pages rather than generalised.

The practical point here is narrow. If you are refurnishing a property for any reason,
screening incoming second-hand items before they go in is the cheapest intervention available
in this entire field, and for a remote property it avoids exactly the sort of problem that is
expensive to come back and deal with. That is covered at
[screening used furniture](/services/k9-bed-bug-detection/used-furniture-screening/).

## What do we need before we come?

Three things, and the third one matters more up here than anywhere.

**What the property is.** Number of spaces, rough layout, how many rooms are furnished. That
determines the search time.

**Who lets us in.** For most properties here that is the owner, which keeps it simple. For a
cabin with a lockbox or a caretaker arrangement, confirm the detail before the date rather
than on the morning.

**Whether anything else can be added to the trip.** Other properties of yours, or other
owners nearby. On the corridor this is a nice-to-have. Up the valley it is the difference
between a proportionate price and an expensive one, and we would rather ask than let somebody
pay for a solo trip they did not need.

## What does the search itself involve?

The same discipline as anywhere, run on properties that are usually more varied than a city
apartment.

The dog works the perimeter of each room first, then the furniture, then the specific
assemblies that matter most: bed frames and headboards, upholstered seating and its
underside, seams and piping, and the junction where skirting meets flooring. When the dog
indicates, the search stops and the handler examines the location directly with a light,
looking for live insects, cast skins, eggs or the dark faecal staining that follows a
harbourage.

Every alert gets a visual confirmation result in one of three states, and they are kept
distinct because they mean different things. **Confirmed**, meaning physical evidence was seen
and photographed. **Not confirmed**, meaning the dog alerted and nothing was visually found;
it stays in the report rather than being quietly removed. **Inconclusive**, meaning access or
conditions prevented a proper look.

Older river-corridor construction produces more inconclusive entries than a modern building,
because there is more that cannot be opened up. That is an honest limit rather than a
failing, and a report that recorded those areas as clear would be worth considerably less
than one that says what it could not see.

## What about outbuildings, cabins and storage?

Worth including, and routinely forgotten.

Rural and recreational property carries more separate structures than urban housing:
detached cabins, bunkhouses, storage sheds holding furniture between seasons, and covered
areas where mattresses and soft furnishings sit for months. Any of those can hold something,
and stored soft furnishings in particular are a common route back into a building that was
cleared.

The pattern we see is a property searched thoroughly, declared clear, refurnished in spring
from items that spent the winter in a shed nobody mentioned, and back where it started. That
is not a failure of the search. It is a scoping gap, and it is avoidable by saying out loud
what is stored where.

So when we scope a property up here we ask what other structures are on it and what is being
kept in them. Screening stored items before they go back into a building is quick, and it is
substantially cheaper than dealing with what follows if they should not have gone in. The
detail is at
[screening used furniture](/services/k9-bed-bug-detection/used-furniture-screening/).

## Working the rest of the county from here

Concrete sits at the far end of the county, and everything else is between here and the
corridor.

[Sedro-Woolley](/service-area/sedro-woolley/) is the nearest town of any size and the
gateway to the SR 20 corridor. [Burlington](/service-area/burlington/) carries the I-5
accommodation cluster. [Mount Vernon](/service-area/mount-vernon/) is the county seat with
the institutional buyers. [La Conner](/service-area/la-conner/) is small historic inns, and
[Anacortes](/service-area/anacortes/) is refineries, ferries and marine trades. Hamilton and
Lyman lie between here and Sedro-Woolley and are covered on the same terms without pages of
their own, because we would rather name them honestly than publish a page we cannot fill.

If your properties are spread across several of those, one trip and one document format beats
a series of separate visits by a wide margin. The programme structure is at
