## What does the statute actually say?

One sentence, containing two exceptions, doing most of the work in this entire area of law.

**RCW 59.18.060(4)** requires a landlord to:

> Provide a reasonable program for the control of infestation by insects, rodents, and
> other pests at the initiation of the tenancy and, except in the case of a single-family
> residence, control infestation during tenancy except where such infestation is caused by
> the tenant.

Read it slowly, because almost every dispute in this area turns on the structure of that
sentence rather than on anything more exotic.

There are **two distinct duties**. The first is to provide a reasonable pest control
programme *at the initiation of the tenancy*. That one applies to every rental, including a
single-family house. The second is to *control infestation during the tenancy*, and that
one carries both carve-outs.

There are **two carve-outs**, and they attach only to the second duty. A single-family
residence is outside the ongoing control duty. And an infestation *caused by the tenant* is
outside it in any property type.

That is the whole of the statewide framework for bed bugs in rental housing. There is no
bed-bug-specific state statute layered on top of it.

## Who has to prove what?

This is the question people actually want answered, and the statute does not answer it
directly.

The duty sits with the landlord as the default. The carve-out for tenant-caused infestation
is an exception to that default, and in practice a party relying on an exception is the
party that needs to make it stand up. A landlord asserting that a tenant caused an
infestation is making a factual claim, and "the tenant must have brought it in" is not a
factual claim. It is an assumption about a common scenario.

The practical difficulty is that bed bugs are genuinely hard to attribute. They arrive with
people and belongings. They move between units through shared walls, floors, voids and
utility chases. An infestation discovered in unit 12 may have originated in unit 12, or in
unit 10, or in a unit on the floor below that was treated eight months ago and never
verified. Nothing about finding them in a unit tells you where they started.

This is why documentation matters more here than legal argument does. A dated record showing
which units were searched, on what date, what was confirmed and what was clear, is the only
thing that turns "we think the tenant caused it" into a position with evidence behind it.
Equally, it is the thing that stops a landlord from pursuing a chargeback that the evidence
does not actually support.

What a canine inspection cannot do is establish origin. No inspection method can. It
establishes distribution and timing. That is the raw material of a causation argument rather
than the argument itself, and anybody telling you a dog can prove who brought bed bugs into
a building is overselling badly.

## What are the tenant's duties?

**RCW 59.18.130** sets them out, and it is the provision behind cost liability.

The section covers the tenant's obligations to keep the premises as clean and sanitary as
conditions permit, to properly dispose of rubbish, to not permit deliberate or negligent
destruction or damage, and related duties. Where an infestation is caused by the tenant, this
is the route by which extermination costs can be pursued.

Two things are worth saying plainly about how this gets used.

First, cleanliness is not the causal variable people assume it is. Bed bugs are not a
sanitation pest. They appear in immaculate homes and in new buildings, because they travel
with people and possessions rather than colonising dirt. A tenant who keeps a spotless unit
can absolutely acquire them, and pointing at housekeeping standards is not the same as
establishing causation.

Second, an internal policy that implies a resident is at fault for reporting has a
predictable and expensive effect: residents stop reporting early. Delayed reporting is the
single most reliable way to turn a one-unit problem into a floor-wide one. If your process
communicates blame, you have built a cost into it.

## What about entry to inspect?

**RCW 59.18.150** governs it, and it is the provision that decides whether a building-wide
response is workable at all.

The landlord has a right of entry, subject to notice requirements. For inspecting a single
unit that is routine. For a coordinated sweep of forty or four hundred units it becomes a
logistics exercise, and it is the part that most often derails a schedule.

The practical failure mode looks like this: a manager books an inspection for the following
week, notices go out late or to the wrong list, and on the day a substantial share of units
cannot be entered. What comes out the other end is not a building sweep. It is a partial
search with a map that looks complete, which is worse than not having done it, because the
document now understates the coverage gap.

Anything requiring access to many units should be scheduled with enough lead time for
notices to be served properly. We book that way deliberately. The detail is at
[entry notice for unit inspections](/compliance/entry-notice-for-unit-inspections/).

## What does the state NOT require?

Worth being explicit, because the misinformation here is persistent and confidently stated.

**There is no statewide disclosure requirement.** Washington does not oblige a landlord to
tell a prospective tenant about a prior infestation. You will find articles and vendor
materials asserting otherwise, sometimes citing a bill number. The bill usually named is HB
1440 from 2019, which is about 60-day notice for rent increases. It has nothing to do with
pests. We flag it because a source willing to invent a statute is a source worth doubting
generally.

**There is no state bed-bug-specific inspection mandate.** Some cities have inspection
programmes; the state does not impose one for pests.

**There is no state-mandated treatment standard or timeline.** The statute requires a
reasonable programme. It does not define the method, the schedule or the standard, which is
precisely why a documented, dated process is how a landlord demonstrates reasonableness.

## How do local rules stack on top?

Unevenly, which catches out anybody managing a portfolio across more than one jurisdiction.

**Seattle** is the strictest and the most explicit. Bed bugs are named specifically in the
municipal code rather than being folded into a general pest category, the Director's Rule
sets standards for extermination programmes and verification, penalties run from $150 to
$500 per day plus inspection charges, and the Rental Registration and Inspection Ordinance
subjects registered rentals to periodic inspection. That is written up at
[Seattle rental bed bug rules](/compliance/seattle-rental-bed-bug-rules/).

**Bellingham** runs a Rental Registration and Safety Inspection Program under BMC 6.15.
Registration before letting, a certificate posted in each unit, inspection on roughly a
three to three-and-a-half year cycle. It is a life-and-fire-safety programme and it does not
impose bed bug obligations. Its relevance is cultural rather than legal: owners there are
already used to third-party inspection and to keeping a compliance file.

**Most of the rest of the state**, including Pierce, Skagit, Snohomish and the smaller
Whatcom cities, runs on RCW 59.18 alone with no municipal layer naming pests.

The consequence for a regional manager is that a single portfolio policy is almost certainly
wrong somewhere. A policy calibrated to Seattle over-documents a Mount Vernon building. A
policy written for Whatcom under-documents the Seattle assets and exposes them to a daily
penalty schedule.

## What does a "reasonable program" look like?

The statute does not define it, and that is the point most operators miss.

A rule that specified a method and a frequency would be easier to follow and easier to
satisfy hollowly. A standard of reasonableness instead asks what you actually did, which
means your evidence of compliance is a record rather than a policy document.

In practice the elements that read as reasonable to a third party are unremarkable. That
there is a written process for receiving and logging a report. That reports are acted on
within a defined period rather than when someone gets to them. That inspection is carried
out by somebody competent, and documented. That neighbouring units are considered rather
than only the reporting unit. That where remediation is carried out, somebody establishes
afterwards whether it worked. And that the whole sequence is dated.

Note what is absent from that list: any particular treatment method, product or vendor. That
is deliberate, and not only because we are not licensed to advise on it. A programme is a
process, and a process is what a reasonableness standard actually examines.

The inverse is worth stating too. A landlord who responds quickly, documents nothing, and
resolves the problem is in a materially worse position two years later than one who
responded at the same speed and wrote it down, because only one of them can demonstrate it.

## What happens when a tenant reports it?

A sequence, and the order matters more than the speed of any individual step.

**Log the report with a date, in writing.** Before anything else. An undated verbal report
establishes nothing about when you knew, and when you knew is the axis most disputes turn
on.

**Establish what was actually observed.** Bites are not a diagnosis. Reactions to bed bug
bites vary enormously between individuals, a substantial share of people show no visible
reaction at all, and a long list of other things bite or irritate skin. What matters is
whether live insects, cast skins, eggs or dark faecal staining were seen. A report of bites
tells you that you have a complaint to resolve, not that you have an infestation.

**Serve entry notice properly under RCW 59.18.150** for whatever inspection follows. A
rushed or defective entry creates a second dispute alongside the first, and it is entirely
avoidable.

**Inspect the reporting unit and a sensible set of neighbours.** This is the step most
commonly skipped and the one that determines whether you are resolving a problem or
following it around a building for a year. Which neighbours depends on how the building is
constructed rather than on a standard diagram; garden-style buildings sharing attics and
utility chases do not follow the neat cross pattern that stacked mid-rise does.

**Only then decide on remediation**, and engage a licensed applicator of your own choosing
if it is warranted.

**Verify afterwards.** An operator confirming its own work has an obvious difficulty, which
is why independent post-treatment verification exists as a distinct service.

## Where do chargeback disputes actually go wrong?

Four failure modes, in rough order of how often we see the consequences.

**Charging before documenting.** A chargeback issued on the assumption that the tenant must
have caused it, with no inspection record behind it, is difficult to sustain if it is
contested and expensive to unwind.

**Treating cleanliness as evidence of causation.** It is not. Bed bugs are not a sanitation
pest and this argument does not survive contact with anyone who knows that.

**Inspecting only the reporting unit.** If the unit next door is also affected and nobody
looked, the causation story is not merely unproven, it is actively undermined by the first
person who does look.

**Discarding the all-clears.** The dated record showing a unit was searched and found clear
is the document that establishes a timeline. It feels worthless on the day because the visit
produced no finding, and it is frequently the most valuable page in the file eighteen months
later.

## Does this apply the same way to every kind of housing?

The statute reads the same. The practical situation does not.

**Student housing.** University-owned rental housing sits under RCW 59.18 like any other
rental, but the operational reality is that an entire year of turnover risk compresses into
two weeks twice a year. Belongings arrive from every market a student came from, which makes
origin tracing meaningless and screening the only sensible response. Parental escalation
also moves considerably faster than any internal work order system, which changes what the
documentation has to be ready for.

**Senior living and care settings.** Residents cannot always report reliably, so
complaint-driven discovery fails as a detection method and screening has to replace it.
There is an additional layer of oversight from licensing and the long-term care ombudsman
programme, and family communication is far easier with a dated third-party document than
with an internal assurance. The absence of chemical exposure during a detection inspection
is a genuine lead consideration in a setting with medically vulnerable residents rather than
a footnote.

**Agricultural workforce housing.** Common across Whatcom and Skagit, and operating under
its own licensing, access and notice arrangements. Occupancy follows the crop calendar
rather than a lease year, the organisation controlling access is frequently not the owner,
and residents may not share a first language with the inspector. It should never be scoped
on conventional multifamily assumptions.

**Single-family rentals.** The carve-out in RCW 59.18.060(4) removes the ongoing duty to
control infestation during the tenancy for this property type. It does not remove the duty
to provide a reasonable programme at the initiation of the tenancy, and it does not mean a
landlord who ignores a report has behaved sensibly. It means the statutory position is
different, which is a narrower point than it is usually reported to be.

**Short-stay lodging.** Not landlord-tenant law at all. The obligations there come from a
different direction entirely, and the operational driver is nightly room revenue rather than
habitability duty.

## What does this mean in practice?

Five things, in the order they tend to matter.

**Respond to reports in writing, with a date.** The date is what makes everything else
usable later. An undated verbal report is not a record of anything.

**Do not decide causation before you have evidence.** The temptation to charge back
immediately is strong and it is how landlords lose disputes they might have won by waiting a
week and documenting properly.

**Screen neighbouring units, not just the reporting one.** One confirmed unit is a question
about the building. Which neighbours depends on construction, and in garden-style buildings
sharing attics and chases the conventional grid is often wrong.

**Serve entry notice properly, with time in hand.** See above. It is the constraint that
most often ruins a sweep.

**Keep the all-clears.** The dated document saying a unit was searched and nothing was found
is the one you will want eighteen months later. It is also the one people throw away,
because at the time it feels like the visit produced nothing.
