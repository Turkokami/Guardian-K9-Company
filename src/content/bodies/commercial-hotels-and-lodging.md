## What is actually being protected here?

Not the room. The rate, the review and the night.

In multifamily the asset at risk is a tenancy and a legal duty. In lodging it is inventory
priced by the night, and that changes every calculation in this field. A room removed from
sale has a precise daily cost you can look up. A guest complaint has a resolution cost and a
publication cost, and the second one keeps running long after the first is settled.

That is the frame worth holding on to, because it explains why lodging operators who take
screening seriously behave differently from every other buyer of this service. They are not
buying reassurance. They are buying the ability to decide quickly, with evidence, at a moment
when the alternative is deciding slowly, on instinct, with a guest in the lobby.

Everything below follows from that: why discretion is operational rather than cosmetic, why
the schedule matters more than the search, and why an independent result is worth more than a
confident one.

## Why is discretion an operational requirement?

Because the visit itself can become the incident.

A branded vehicle outside the entrance, a technician in a marked uniform in the corridor, or
a dog working a floor while guests are checking in are all pieces of information, and in a
hotel information travels faster than facts. A guest who sees a detection dog and draws a
conclusion does not wait for the result, and neither does the review they write.

So the default arrangement is unmarked. No branded vehicle, no branded clothing, access
coordinated through whoever you nominate rather than through the front desk, and scheduling
built around occupancy, including after-hours and overnight windows where that is what works.

There is a second-order benefit that operators notice within a few visits. When screening is
routine, unremarkable and invisible, staff stop treating it as an emergency signal. A
programme that looks like maintenance gets cooperation. A programme that looks like a crisis
response gets avoided, and avoided screening is the expensive kind.

## What does the schedule need to look like?

Layered, because one pattern cannot serve both the routine and the incident.

**A rotating sample.** A defined share of rooms on a repeating cycle, chosen systematically
rather than by availability. This is what turns a set of unrelated incidents into a baseline
you can reason from, and it is the layer most properties skip because nothing appears to
prompt it.

**Seasonal full passes.** Before and after your heaviest occupancy period, whenever that
falls. Before, because a room can still be held. After, because a high-turnover stretch
should not roll into the rest of the year unexamined. In Washington that peak differs sharply
by market: the [Skagit Valley](/service-area/mount-vernon/) tulip season in April, the
[Anacortes](/service-area/anacortes/) refinery turnaround calendar, cruise season in Seattle,
and the summer through-traffic that fills the
[I-5 corridor](/service-area/burlington/).

**Event-driven screening.** A specific complaint, a room with a history, or a block of rooms
that housed a single high-mobility group. This is the reactive layer, and it works properly
only when the first two exist, because otherwise every finding is context-free.

**Verification.** After any remediation, before the room returns to inventory. Covered below.

The rotation is the part worth insisting on. A systematic rotation across a year tells you
which floors and room types produce findings and which do not. Screening whichever rooms are
free tells you almost nothing, because the sample selects itself and the rooms that are never
free are exactly the ones being slept in most.

## What makes lodging harder to screen than housing?

Three things, and the first is the one nobody plans for.

**The stay is short.** For a guest to report anything they have to be bitten, react visibly,
recognise the reaction, and say so before checking out. A substantial share of people show no
visible reaction at all, and reactions that do appear are frequently delayed by days. A guest
staying one night has almost no chance of completing that sequence, which means
complaint-driven discovery under-reports badly and unevenly. Highway and corridor properties
running on one and two night stays are the extreme case.

**The occupant changes constantly and the room does not.** In an apartment building the
occupant is stable and introduction is comparatively rare. In lodging the room is stable and
the occupant changes continuously, which is where the introduction opportunity actually
lives. That is a statement about volume of turnover, not about guests.

**Housekeeping sees everything and is told nothing.** Your cleaners enter every room, every
changeover, far more often than any inspector will. They are the cheapest detection capacity
in the building and most properties never tell them what to look at.

That last one is worth fixing regardless of whether you ever hire us. Dark spotting that has
bled into fabric along mattress seams and piping; translucent cast skins tucked into
crevices; live insects about the size of an apple seed, flat and reddish-brown; and above
all, the back of the headboard and the wall behind it, which is the most commonly skipped
surface in any changeover. Photograph a find, note the room and date, leave it in place, and
escalate. Cleaning it away destroys the evidence that would have established what it was.

## Why does verification matter more here than anywhere?

Because the decision it supports is when to sell the room again.

After remediation, somebody has to decide the room is fit to return to inventory. If that
decision rests on the assurance of the company that performed the work, it rests on the least
independent evidence available. Nobody has to be dishonest for that to be uncomfortable: a
finding at verification means a failed job and a callback, which is a great deal of pressure
to put on a marginal judgement about ambiguous staining.

An independent check removes that variable. It also produces a cleaner file: a dated
remediation record followed by a dated independent verification closes an episode, where a
remediation record alone leaves it open indefinitely.

One honest caveat that most providers skip. A treated room is genuinely harder to read than
an untreated one, because residues persist after a population is gone. Cast skins, old
staining and dead insects in voids can all remain. An alert in a recently treated room is
therefore not automatic evidence that remediation failed, and a report that presents it that
way is overreaching. That is exactly why unconfirmed alerts stay in our documents as
unconfirmed rather than being resolved in whichever direction suits the reader. The detail is
at [post-treatment verification](/services/bed-bug-verification-inspection/).

## What does the record have to survive?

Being read, months later, by somebody with an interest in disputing it.

A guest claim, a chargeback, an insurer's question, an OTA dispute or a franchise audit can
all arrive long after everyone involved has forgotten the visit. At that point the document
is all that exists, and it will be read by a stranger looking for the gap between what it
says and what it implies.

So each room appears with a result. Each alert is mapped to its location within the room
rather than counted. Each alert carries a visual confirmation result in one of three states,
kept distinct because they mean different things: **confirmed**, where physical evidence was
seen and photographed; **not confirmed**, where the dog alerted and nothing was found;
**inconclusive**, where access or conditions prevented a proper look. Rooms that could not be
entered are listed by number rather than quietly omitted.

That last item is the one that separates a survey from a flattering document. A report
covering forty-two rooms of a fifty-room property, with no mention of the other eight,
invites a reader to conclude the property was screened. It was not. The standard is set out
at [what makes a report defensible](/compliance/what-a-defensible-report-contains/).

## Where does the law sit for lodging?

Somewhere different from tenancy, which catches out mixed operators.

A guest room is not a tenancy. **RCW 59.18**, the residential landlord-tenant framework that
governs apartments, is not the governing law for a nightly let, so the habitability duty,
entry notice and tenant-causation carve-outs described at
[who is responsible under Washington landlord-tenant law](/compliance/washington-landlord-tenant-bed-bugs/)
do not transfer. Washington regulates transient accommodation through a separate licensing
route, and brand standards frequently impose documentation obligations well beyond anything
statutory.

The practical consequence is for operators holding both. A property company with long-term
rentals and short-stay units is working under two frameworks at once, and a single internal
policy written for one will be wrong for the other. Extended-stay properties sit closest to
the line and are worth taking advice on specifically.

None of that changes what we do. It changes what your obligations are around it, and it is
worth knowing which set you are operating under before an incident rather than during one.

## What does an incident actually cost?

Work outwards from the invoice and the real number appears.

**The room, out of service.** The only figure most operators put in the budget, and the
smallest of the set. It is also the easiest to calculate: nightly rate times nights held.

**The adjoining rooms.** A careful operator holds neighbours until they have been checked.
In a small property that can be a meaningful share of inventory; in a large one it is a floor
plan question. Either way it multiplies the first number rather than adding to it.

**The guest.** Moving them, refunding them, or compensating them, plus the staff time spent
on the conversation. In a full market, moving a guest means paying somebody else's rate.

**The escalation.** If the complaint reaches an OTA, a franchise inspector or a brand
standards team, the cost stops being about one room and becomes about process. What is asked
for at that point is almost always documentation, and its absence is more damaging than the
original finding.

**The review.** This is the one that behaves unlike every other cost in the list. A
remediation invoice is paid once. A published review keeps working against the property for
as long as it is displayed, in front of exactly the audience deciding whether to book, and it
is effectively impossible to retract.

Set a rotating screening programme against that and the comparison is not close. What makes
it hard is that the screening cost is visible and budgeted while most of the incident cost is
diffuse and absorbed, so the two rarely get compared honestly. Operators who have been
through a bad episode need no persuading. The argument is for everyone who has not.

## What about extended-stay and corporate housing?

A hybrid, and worth scoping as its own thing.

Extended-stay properties, corporate apartments and relocation units sit between lodging and
tenancy. The stay is long enough that occupants unpack, bring more belongings and settle in,
which makes each room more like a residence in terms of what has to be searched. But turnover
is still far faster than a conventional lease, so the introduction frequency stays closer to
lodging.

That combination produces the worst of both patterns: apartment-scale search time on a
lodging-scale turnover cycle. It is also the segment where the legal position is least
obvious, because a long enough stay may look more like a tenancy than a licence, which is a
question for your advisers rather than for us.

Practically, we scope these on the residential model — more time per unit, furnished search,
storage and closets included — while screening on the lodging rhythm, which usually means
building the programme around turn dates rather than around a calendar. Corporate and
relocation stock in markets like [Bellevue](/service-area/bellevue/) turns over fast enough
that turnover screening does most of the work; the mechanics are at
[move-in and move-out screening](/services/turnover-screening/).

## What we will not tell you

That a clean result certifies the property.

It records what was searched, on what date, and that nothing was found. That is genuinely
useful and it is a narrower claim than the one operators sometimes want. No screening method
proves a negative, and the published field research on canine detection shows real
variability between working teams, which is why we publish the handler, the dog, the
certifying body and the certification dates rather than a percentage. The figures and the
sources are at [detection accuracy](/detection-accuracy/).

We will also not tell you which treatment to buy. That is separately licensed work in
Washington and we do not hold that licence. What we will do is hand whoever you engage a
precise map of where confirmed evidence was found, which makes their scoping sharper and
their quote more honest.

And we will not tell you that screening eliminates the problem. It finds things earlier, when
they are cheaper and more contained. Given how this industry's costs actually behave, earlier
is most of the value there is.
