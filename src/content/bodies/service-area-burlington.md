## Why is Burlington the county's accommodation problem?

Because the beds and the residents are in different towns.

Skagit County's population centre is [Mount Vernon](/service-area/mount-vernon/), which holds
the county seat, the courts, the hospital and most of the conventional apartment stock.
Burlington holds the retail and lodging cluster along the Interstate 5 corridor. That split
is unusual, and it has a direct consequence for anybody deciding where screening effort
belongs: the transient accommodation risk in this county concentrates here, while the
long-term residential risk sits a few miles south.

For a regional manager holding property in both, that means two different screening
rationales in one county. A Mount Vernon apartment building is screened on a tenancy rhythm:
turnover, complaints, adjoining units. A Burlington motel or extended-stay property is
screened on an occupancy rhythm, which is faster, more continuous and considerably less
forgiving.

We work Burlington on the regular schedule. Skagit is a prime work zone, and a property
directly off the freeway is among the easiest places in the county for us to reach, which is
the opposite of how it looks to a company operating out of the Puget Sound corridor.

## What makes short-stay accommodation the hardest thing to screen?

The stay length, and specifically the one and two night stay.

Consider what has to happen for a guest to report anything. They have to be bitten, they have
to react visibly, they have to recognise what the reaction is, and they have to be willing to
say so before they leave. A substantial share of people show no visible reaction at all, and
reactions that do appear are often delayed by days. A guest who arrives at nine in the
evening and leaves at seven the next morning has no realistic chance of completing that chain
even if something is present.

Highway corridor accommodation runs on precisely that pattern. Freight traffic, families
breaking a long drive, contractors between job sites, and travellers heading north or south
on I-5 all produce short stays in volume. The room turns again within hours.

The result is that complaint-driven discovery, which is already unreliable, is close to
useless in this setting. By the time somebody reports something, the room has hosted a long
sequence of other people and there is no way to reconstruct anything. That is not an argument
for alarm. It is an argument for screening on a schedule, because the schedule is the only
thing in this environment that is actually under your control.

The setting is covered at [hotels and lodging](/commercial/hotels-and-lodging/).

## When should a corridor property actually be screened?

On a cadence, sized to the room count, and timed around the seasonal peaks rather than
through them.

Two seasonal factors dominate here and neither is about the weather. The Skagit Valley Tulip
Festival runs through April and pulls very heavy visitor volume into the valley, and
Burlington's corridor accommodation absorbs a large share of it. Summer through-traffic on
I-5 is the other. Both mean the same thing operationally: during the peak, no room can be
taken out of service, so the peak is the worst possible time to find something.

The useful pattern is to screen before the season starts, when a room can still be held, and
again after it ends, so a heavy period does not roll into the rest of the year undetected.
Between those, a rotating sample of rooms rather than the whole property keeps the cost
proportionate while still producing a real picture of the building over a year.

The rotation matters more than the sample size. A genuinely systematic rotation tells you
something about the property. Screening whichever rooms happen to be free tells you almost
nothing, because the sample selects itself.

## Does the retail and workforce housing change the residential picture?

It raises mobility, which raises the value of screening at turnover.

Retail, hospitality and freight employment support a workforce rental segment with
higher-than-average tenant movement. High mobility is not a statement about the people
involved; it is a statement about how many times a year a unit changes hands, and each change
is both an introduction opportunity and a screening opportunity.

That makes turnover the single most efficient moment to look. The unit is empty, no entry
notice arises under **RCW 59.18.150**, there is no occupant to schedule around, nothing is
obstructing the search, and almost none of the unit ends up recorded as inconclusive.
Coverage in an empty unit is better than coverage in an occupied one, which makes the result
more useful as baseline data as well as cheaper to obtain.

For a building with any history, screening every turn is defensible. For one without,
screening a systematic share of turns produces a usable picture at a fraction of the cost.
The mechanics are at [move-in and move-out screening](/services/turnover-screening/).

## What governs a rental here?

State law, with nothing added locally.

**RCW 59.18.060(4)** requires a landlord to provide a reasonable pest control programme at
the initiation of a tenancy and, other than in a single-family residence, to control
infestation during the tenancy except where the tenant caused it. **RCW 59.18.130** is the
route to tenant cost liability where causation is established. **RCW 59.18.150** requires at
least two days written notice to enter and inspect.

Burlington adds no municipal provision naming bed bugs, and neither does the rest of Skagit.
Seattle is the outlier, with bed bugs named explicitly and penalties running from $150 to
$500 per day, and Bellingham runs a rental registration and safety inspection programme
under BMC 6.15 that is built around life and fire safety rather than pests.

Worth flagging for mixed portfolios: a motel room is not a tenancy, and landlord-tenant law
is not the framework governing it. An operator holding both long-term rentals and short-stay
accommodation in this corridor is working under two different sets of obligations at once,
and a single internal policy written for one will be wrong for the other. The tenancy side is
summarised at
[who is responsible under Washington landlord-tenant law](/compliance/washington-landlord-tenant-bed-bugs/).

## What does the river mean for a property here?

Not the water itself. What follows it.

Burlington sits on the Skagit valley floor and shares the flood exposure of the towns around
it. The relevance to this work is indirect but real: remediation after a water event brings
replacement furnishings into buildings, often quickly and often second-hand or donated, and
households displaced by flooding move through temporary accommodation. Displacement,
temporary housing and replaced furniture are all documented introduction pathways, and none
of them appears in a maintenance record.

The sharpest version of that story in our service area is a county north, where the November
2021 Nooksack flooding damaged the overwhelming majority of structures in Sumas and displaced
hundreds of people. That is written up honestly on the [Sumas](/service-area/sumas/) and
[Everson](/service-area/everson/) pages rather than generalised across the region.

The practical point for Burlington is narrower. If a property has taken water recently, or is
being refurnished for any reason, screening incoming second-hand items before they go in is
the cheapest intervention available in this entire field. That is covered at
[screening used furniture](/services/k9-bed-bug-detection/used-furniture-screening/).

## Are the geese here a real problem?

On corridor commercial ground with water on it, routinely.

Resident Canada geese settle where there is mown grass, clear sightlines and water nearby.
Commercial development along the corridor supplies all three, usually in the form of
stormwater retention ponds ringed by maintained turf and crossed daily by staff and
customers. The result is fouled walkways and entrances, a slip exposure across ground people
have to walk, and turf that stops being usable for what it was landscaped for.

Washington Department of Fish and Wildlife's Living with Wildlife guidance names
handler-directed dogs as the method of choice for large open areas including corporate parks
and playing fields. The same guidance is explicit that dogs can be inappropriate near
roadways, in continuously used public areas and on large water bodies, and a corridor site
sitting hard against a freeway is exactly where that caution applies. We will say when a site
is the wrong shape for this method.

What we do is handler-directed hazing of resident Canada geese: no contact, no injury, no
nests or eggs disturbed. Canada geese are federally protected under the Migratory Bird Treaty
Act and the federal definition of take at 50 CFR 10.12 includes pursuit, which is why how the
work is run and recorded is a legal question rather than a presentational one. Registration
under the Resident Canada Goose Nest and Egg Depredation Order at 50 CFR 21.162 belongs to
the landowner rather than to a vendor, so nest and egg work is not ours to offer, and hazing
pauses during the flightless molt as published policy.

One clarification that matters in this valley specifically: the vast winter flocks Skagit is
known for are **snow geese**, a different population managed under a different framework. We
do not offer snow goose work on farmland. The legal picture is at
[Canada goose law in Washington](/compliance/canada-goose-law-washington/).

## What does an accommodation job look like in practice?

A room count, a changeover window, and a document at the end.

We scope from the room count and the property layout rather than quoting a flat rate. What
determines the day is how many rooms can actually be entered and when, which for
accommodation means working around occupancy rather than around notice periods. That is
usually easier than a residential building and considerably less flexible, because the window
between checkout and the next arrival is short and immovable.

Discretion is an operational requirement rather than a courtesy. For public-facing property we
work without marked vehicles or branded clothing and coordinate access through whoever you
nominate. A screening visit that looks like an incident to the guests in the corridor is
worse than useless.

What comes out is the same document format every other client receives: every alert location
recorded against the room, the visual confirmation result for each as confirmed, not confirmed
or inconclusive, photographs where confirmation was possible, and a written all-clear where
nothing was found stating what was searched and what was not. Rooms that could not be entered
are listed rather than omitted. The standard is at
[what makes a report defensible](/compliance/what-a-defensible-report-contains/).

## What does a complaint actually cost a corridor property?

More than the remediation, and in a shape most operators do not budget for.

The invoice from an applicator is the visible number and usually the smallest one. The larger
costs are the nights the room cannot be sold while the matter is resolved, the rooms either
side that a cautious operator will also hold, the staff time spent on the guest, any refund
or goodwill gesture, and a review that stays visible on the booking platforms long after the
room is fine again.

That last item is the one that behaves differently from every other cost in this field. A
remediation invoice is paid once. A review keeps working against the property for as long as
it is displayed, and it is visible to precisely the audience deciding whether to book.

Against that, a scheduled screening pass across a sample of rooms is a small and predictable
number. It is also the only spending in this area that can be planned rather than absorbed
when it happens.

None of that is an argument that every corridor property needs a full programme. It is an
argument for doing the arithmetic honestly, because operators comparing a screening quote
against the applicator's invoice alone are comparing it against the wrong figure.

## What should housekeeping be told to look at?

Four things, and none of them need a dog.

Housekeeping staff are in every room, every changeover, far more often than any inspector
will ever be. They are the cheapest detection capacity a property has and most operators
never tell them what to look for.

**Dark spotting along seams.** Small marks that have bled slightly into the fabric rather
than sitting on top of it, clustered along mattress seams and piping, where skirting meets
flooring, and in the folds of upholstered furniture. This is the most commonly found evidence
in practice.

**Cast skins.** Translucent hollow shells the shape of the insect, usually tucked into a
crevice. They persist long after the insect has gone.

**Live insects.** About the size of an apple seed, flat and reddish-brown, rounder and darker
after feeding. Immature stages are smaller, paler and hard to see against light fabric.

**The back of the headboard and the wall behind it.** The single most commonly skipped
surface in any changeover, and among the most productive places to look.

What to do about a find is straightforward: photograph it, note the room and the date, leave
it in place, and tell whoever makes the decisions. Cleaning it away removes the evidence that
would have let somebody establish what it actually was. And a report from a housekeeper is a
reason to inspect rather than a reason to book remediation, because plenty of dark specks are
not what they look like.
