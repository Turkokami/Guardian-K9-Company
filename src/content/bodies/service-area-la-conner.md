## Who actually buys this in La Conner?

Owners, not facilities departments.

La Conner's accommodation base is small independent inns, bed and breakfasts and vacation
rentals rather than branded hotels or apartment blocks. That single fact drives almost
everything about how this work runs here, and it is the thing a template location page would
miss entirely.

In a hotel there is a director of housekeeping, a documented incident procedure, a pest
contractor already under agreement, and somebody whose job includes reading an inspection
report. In a six-room inn there is an owner who also does the breakfast, the bookings and the
laundry, and who will be the person reading whatever we write.

Two consequences follow. The report has to be legible to somebody who does not do this for a
living, which is a writing problem rather than an inspection one. And the scope conversation
has to be honest about what a single visit establishes, because an owner without a facilities
background is more likely to hear "all clear" as "permanently fine" unless the document says
otherwise plainly.

We work La Conner on the regular schedule. Skagit is a prime work zone and this is a short
run from the corridor, which is not how it looks to a company based two counties south.

## Why does a small property need this more than a large one?

Because there is no slack anywhere in the system.

A hundred-room hotel that takes a room out of service loses one per cent of its inventory and
absorbs the disruption inside an existing process. A six-room inn taking one room out loses
close to seventeen per cent of everything it has to sell, during a season it depends on, with
no procedure to fall back on and usually no second property to move a guest to.

The reputational exposure is also sharper. Small independent accommodation lives and dies on
reviews, and there are fewer of them, so a single bad one carries proportionally more weight
and stays visible longer. The volume of positive reviews that would bury it at a large
property simply is not there.

Against that, the screening pass itself is small. Six or eight rooms is a short visit rather
than a project. It is one of the few places in this field where the buyer with the least
resources gets the most protection per pound spent, and it is exactly the buyer least likely
to have been told so, because the industry's attention goes where the room counts are.

The setting generally is at [hotels and lodging](/commercial/hotels-and-lodging/).

## When should a seasonal property be screened?

On the shoulders, twice, and never in the middle.

Two seasons dominate here. The Skagit Valley Tulip Festival runs through April and pulls very
heavy visitor volume onto the valley floor, and a town this small absorbs a disproportionate
share of it relative to its size. Summer is the second, running through the visitor months
and the events calendar.

During either peak, every room is committed and none can be held. That makes the peak the
worst possible moment to discover something, because the only options are to take revenue out
of service at the point it is worth most, or to keep selling a room you now have doubts
about.

So the useful pattern is before and after. Before the season, anything found is dealt with
while a room can still be held and while there is time to arrange remediation properly. After
the season, a heavy turnover period does not roll straight into the next year undetected, and
the property enters its quiet months with a dated baseline.

That rhythm costs the same as screening at a random point in the year. It just requires the
booking to be made at the right moment, which is free.

## What does historic fabric change about the search?

It slows the search down and limits what can be opened.

The building stock here is historic and generally restored rather than replaced, which is
most of the town's commercial appeal. It also means trim, skirting voids, floor junctions and
wall assemblies of a kind that offer far more harbourage than modern sealed construction, and
a good deal more of them.

The genuine constraint is that restoration limits what can be opened up to look. In a modern
building a piece of trim can be lifted and replaced without consequence. In a restored
historic room it frequently cannot, and no inspector should be pulling at joinery in a
property whose fabric is the product.

Our answer to that is the same discipline we apply everywhere, which matters more here than
usual: where access or conditions prevent a proper look, the area is recorded as
**inconclusive** rather than clear. That word appearing in a report on a historic property is
not a failure of the inspection. It is an accurate description of a real limit, and writing
"clear" instead would be the single most damaging thing we could do to an owner who then
relies on it.

## What about the Swinomish Indian Tribal Community?

A neighbouring sovereign government, and the answer to "who do we ask" is different there.

The Swinomish Indian Tribal Community sits across the channel and is a sovereign government
with its own procurement, its own housing authority and its own jurisdiction. County and city
process do not carry across. Work on tribal land is arranged with that government directly.

We state this plainly because assuming otherwise is both disrespectful and practically
useless: a vendor arriving with a county-shaped process wastes everybody's time. If you manage
property adjacent to or in partnership with the Community, the access and authorisation
questions need answering before a date is set rather than on the morning.

The same principle applies to any shared or ambiguous ground. Establishing who controls a
property, and who is authorised to admit an inspector to it, is a scoping question we ask
before we ask about the calendar.

## What governs a rental here?

State law for tenancies, and something different entirely for short stays.


La Conner adds no municipal provision naming bed bugs, and neither does the rest of Skagit.
Seattle is the statewide outlier, with bed bugs named explicitly and penalties running from
$150 to $500 per day.

The distinction that matters most here is that **a guest room is not a tenancy**.
Landlord-tenant law is not the framework governing a nightly let or an inn room, and an owner
holding both a long-term rental and short-stay accommodation is operating under two different
sets of obligations at once. Small mixed operators frequently do not realise that, and a
single internal policy written for one will be wrong for the other. For the tenancy half, see
[who is responsible under Washington landlord-tenant law](/compliance/washington-landlord-tenant-bed-bugs/).

## What does the report do for a small operator?

Answers a guest complaint that would otherwise be unanswerable in either direction.

The characteristic problem in small accommodation is a guest asserting they were bitten, with
no way for the owner to confirm or refute it. Bites are not a diagnosis: reactions vary
enormously, a substantial share of people show no visible reaction at all, and plenty of other
things bite or irritate skin. Without an inspection record the owner is in the worst possible
position, unable to defend the room or to act on it with confidence, and usually deciding
under pressure with a review pending.

A dated search with a written result changes that both ways. Where nothing is found, you have
a document from an independent party stating what was searched, on what date, and that
nothing was present. Where something is found, you know before the review is written rather
than after, and you can act while you still have the initiative.

Both outcomes are worth the visit, and the second is not a failure of the service. What the
report will not do is prove absence in general, or establish who brought something in, and we
would rather say that than let a small owner rely on it for more than it can carry. The
standard is at
[what makes a report defensible](/compliance/what-a-defensible-report-contains/).

## What should a changeover be looking for?

Four things, and in a small property the owner usually is the housekeeping.

**Dark spotting along seams.** Marks that have bled slightly into fabric rather than sitting
on top of it, clustered along mattress seams and piping, where skirting meets flooring, and in
the folds of upholstered furniture. This is the most commonly found evidence in practice.

**Cast skins.** Translucent hollow shells the shape of the insect, tucked into a crevice.
They persist long after the insect has gone.

**Live insects.** About the size of an apple seed, flat, reddish-brown, rounder and darker
after feeding. Immature stages are smaller, paler and hard to see against light fabric.

**The back of the headboard and the wall behind it.** The most commonly skipped surface in any
changeover and among the most productive to check.

If you find something, photograph it, note the room and date, leave it in place, and get it
looked at. Cleaning it away removes the evidence somebody would need to establish what it
actually was, and plenty of dark specks are not what they look like. A find is a reason to
inspect rather than a reason to book remediation.

## What does a finding actually cost a six-room inn?

Far more than the remediation, and in a shape that does not appear on any invoice.

The applicator's bill is the visible number and usually the smallest one. Work outwards from
it and the real figure appears.

**The room, out of service.** For a six-room property that is roughly a sixth of everything
you have to sell, for as long as the matter takes. In peak season that is the most expensive
inventory of the year.

**The rooms either side.** A careful owner will usually hold neighbouring rooms until they
have been checked, which in a small building can mean half the property.

**The guests already booked.** Moving them means finding somewhere else in a town that is
also full, and paying the difference.

**The review.** This is the one that behaves differently from every other cost. A remediation
invoice is paid once. A review keeps working against a small independent property for as long
as it is displayed, in front of exactly the people deciding whether to book, and there are
fewer positive reviews to bury it than a large hotel would have.

Set a pre-season screening pass against that and the arithmetic is not close. A short visit
across six or eight rooms in a quiet week is a small, predictable, plannable number. It is
also the only spending in this area you can choose the timing of.

None of which means every small property needs a programme. It means the comparison worth
making is against the full cost of the bad version, not against the applicator's quote, and
small operators consistently compare against the wrong figure because it is the only one
anybody has shown them.

## Working the rest of the county from here

La Conner is close enough to everything in the valley that it groups well into a single trip.

[Burlington](/service-area/burlington/) carries the corridor accommodation cluster.

small-owner rental market. [Concrete](/service-area/concrete/) is upper valley with genuine
travel time.

For several small properties, booking them into one visit prices considerably better per
property than separate trips, because the fixed cost of getting there is spread across all of
them. If you know other operators in town who want the same thing, say so when you call and
we will schedule accordingly.

marketing it costs us.
