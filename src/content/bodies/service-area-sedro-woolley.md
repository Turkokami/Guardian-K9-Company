## What kind of market is this?

A small one made of individual owners, which changes the job more than the geography does.

Sedro-Woolley is a former mill and logging town with an older housing stock and a rental
market that is not dominated by regional management companies. Most of the property here is
held by people who own a few units rather than a portfolio, alongside a set of institutional
and publicly held sites that operate on entirely different rules.

That matters for a practical reason. A regional manager has a process: a form for logging a
tenant report, a contractor already under agreement, somebody whose job is documentation, and
a lawyer on retainer. A person who owns three units has none of that, and when a resident
reports something the first decision they make is usually the expensive one.

So the value of what we produce is inverted here. A large operator buys our report to slot
into an existing file. A small owner buys it because it is the only file they have, and
because it is the thing that tells them whether they have a problem at all before they spend
money on the assumption that they do.

We work Sedro-Woolley on the regular schedule. Skagit is a prime work zone and this is a
short run off the corridor, which is not how it looks to a company operating out of the
Puget Sound corridor.

## What does the mill-era housing stock change?

The time a proper search takes, and therefore the price. Nothing about the odds.

Early-century wood-frame construction offers far more harbourage than anything modern. Trim
and skirting with voids behind them, floor and wall junctions that were never sealed, picture
rails, deep window casings, and assemblies that have been altered several times over a
century. Every one of those is a place to search, and there are simply more of them than in a
building put up in 2015.

The consequence is that a nominally identical unit takes longer here than on the corridor.
Scoping assumptions carried over from newer stock will be wrong, and the overrun turns up at
the end of the day when the dog is tired and the searching is at its worst. We would rather
put the extra time in the quote.

It is worth being explicit about what this does not mean. Older buildings are not more likely
to have bed bugs. They arrive with people and belongings, not with buildings, and a new house
takes them as readily as an old one. What age changes is how far they can move once inside,
how many places they can be, and how long it takes to rule a room out. That is a cost
question, and it is regularly misrepresented as a risk question by people selling something.

Clutter matters more than either, and more than floor area. A heavily occupied small unit can
take longer than a large sparse one, and where clutter or access prevents a proper search the
space is recorded as inconclusive rather than clear.

## What governs a rental here?

State law, with nothing added locally.

**RCW 59.18.060(4)** requires a landlord to provide a reasonable pest control programme at
the initiation of a tenancy and, other than in a single-family residence, to control
infestation during the tenancy except where the tenant caused it. **RCW 59.18.130** sets out
tenant duties and is the route to cost liability where the tenant is the cause.
**RCW 59.18.150** requires at least two days written notice to enter and inspect.

Sedro-Woolley adds no municipal provision naming bed bugs, and neither does the rest of
Skagit. Seattle is the statewide outlier, with bed bugs named explicitly in its housing code
and penalties running from $150 to $500 per day. Bellingham operates a rental registration
and safety inspection programme under BMC 6.15, but that is built around life and fire safety
and does not extend here.

The single-family carve-out is worth reading carefully if you own houses rather than
apartments, which many owners here do. It removes the ongoing duty to control infestation
during the tenancy for that property type. It does not remove the duty to provide a
reasonable programme at the initiation of the tenancy, and it does not mean ignoring a report
is a sensible commercial decision. The statutory position is at
[who is responsible under Washington landlord-tenant law](/compliance/washington-landlord-tenant-bed-bugs/).

## What should a small owner actually do with a tenant report?

Slow down, in a specific order, before spending anything.

**Write it down with a date.** Before anything else. An undated verbal report establishes
nothing about when you knew, and when you knew is what most disputes turn on.

**Ask what was seen, not what was felt.** Bites are not a diagnosis. Reactions vary
enormously between people, a substantial share of people show no visible reaction at all, and
a long list of other things bite or irritate skin. What matters is whether live insects, cast
skins, eggs or dark faecal staining were seen.

**Serve entry notice properly** under RCW 59.18.150 for whatever inspection follows. A rushed
entry creates a second dispute alongside the first, and it is entirely avoidable.

**Screen the reporting unit and its neighbours**, not the reporting unit alone. In a duplex or
a converted house the shared assemblies are not always obvious, and the unit that matters may
be one you would not have listed.

**Only then decide about remediation**, and engage a licensed applicator of your own choosing
if it is warranted.

The step small owners skip is the fourth one, and it is the step that decides whether this is
a contained problem or one that circulates through the building for a year. There is a
dedicated piece at
[bites but no bed bugs found](/services/k9-bed-bug-detection/bites-but-no-evidence/).

## What about the institutional and publicly held property?

Different procurement, different timescale, higher documentation expectations.

The former Northern State Hospital campus is a large institutional site in public ownership,
and it is not the only publicly held property in the area. Work on that kind of site is
arranged with a public body rather than a landlord, which means a specification, an
authorisation chain, and usually somebody whose job is to find the weakness in whatever
document you hand over.

That suits how we already work. Every report is dated and signed by the handler who performed
the inspection. Every alert carries its visual confirmation result as confirmed, not confirmed
or inconclusive. Unconfirmed alerts stay in the document rather than being quietly removed,
because a report in which every alert was confirmed should raise an eyebrow rather than
reassure. And anything we could not enter is listed rather than omitted.

The practical advice for a public body is to start earlier than feels necessary. The
inspection itself is quick; the authorisation is not. The standard the document is held to is
at [what makes a report defensible](/compliance/what-a-defensible-report-contains/).

## Does the highway season matter?

To lodging, yes. To residential property, not really.

Sedro-Woolley sits at the western end of the State Route 20 corridor, the gateway to the
North Cascades Highway. Accommodation here tracks the highway season rather than the calendar
year, which concentrates short-stay turnover into the months the pass is open and thins it
out afterwards.

For an accommodation operator that produces the same pattern we describe everywhere in the
northern counties: screen on the shoulders of the season rather than through it, because
during the peak no room can be taken out of service and that is exactly when you least want
an answer. Before the season, anything found can be dealt with while a room can still be
held. After it, a heavy turnover period does not roll into the rest of the year undetected.

For residential landlords the highway season is largely irrelevant, and it would be
misleading to imply otherwise. Bed bugs live indoors in heated buildings and there is no
month in which an inspection is pointless. What is seasonal is the occupancy rhythm of
specific property types, not the insect.

## Where does the river fit?

Indirectly, through what follows a water event rather than the water itself.

Sedro-Woolley sits on the Skagit and shares the valley's flood exposure. The relevance here is
the same as everywhere else in the valley: remediation brings replacement furnishings into
buildings, often quickly and often second-hand or donated, and displaced households move
through temporary accommodation. Displacement, temporary housing and replaced furniture are
all documented introduction pathways, and none of them shows up in a maintenance record.

The sharpest version of that story in our service area is a county north, where the November
2021 Nooksack flooding damaged the overwhelming majority of structures in Sumas and displaced
hundreds of people. That is written up on the [Sumas](/service-area/sumas/) and
[Everson](/service-area/everson/) pages rather than generalised across the region.

For an owner refurnishing after any water event, screening incoming second-hand items before
they enter a building is the cheapest intervention available in this entire field. That is
covered at
[screening used furniture](/services/k9-bed-bug-detection/used-furniture-screening/).

## What do we need before we arrive?

Four things, and for a small owner they are simpler than they sound.

**A unit list.** How many spaces, roughly how they are laid out, and how many rooms are
furnished. Search time follows from that.

**Who lets us in.** For a small owner this is usually you, which removes most of the
complexity larger properties carry. For a duplex or converted house with separate tenancies it
is worth confirming who can admit us to which part.

**Notice served, with time in hand.** Two days is the statutory floor rather than a plan.
Serving on a Friday for a Monday visit technically clears it and leaves no room for a tenant
to raise a scheduling conflict.

**Anything you already know.** Previous inspections, previous treatment, a recent water event,
second-hand furniture that came in. None of that changes what we do; it changes what the
result means, and it belongs in the record either way.

## Why do smaller markets get worse service, and what do we do about it?

Because of how routes are planned, and it is worth being blunt about the mechanics.

A company operating out of the Puget Sound corridor plans its week around density. A day
spent in Seattle or Everett fills with work inside a short radius. A day spent up here does
not, so the town gets served when somebody is already passing, which means the date moves
whenever something closer appears. Nobody decides to treat a small market badly. It falls out
of the arithmetic.

The consequences are familiar to anybody who owns property here. Long lead times. Dates that
shift twice before anybody arrives. A quote that assumes a corridor building and then gets
revised once the actual property is seen. And a reluctance to come back for anything small.

Our answer is structural rather than a promise. Whatcom and Skagit are the prime work zones,
so the schedule is built around this county rather than around a city two hours south, and a
short run off the corridor is routine work instead of a detour that has to be justified.

That does not make us better at searching a building. It makes the date we give you a date
that holds, which for a small owner is most of what was actually missing. It also means we
will come back for one unit, which sounds trivial until you have tried to get somebody to.

There is a limit worth stating alongside that. Being nearby does not make us fast at
everything: building-wide work still needs entry notice served properly under RCW 59.18.150,
and that timeline is set by your process rather than our calendar. Proximity fixes travel, not
notice periods.
