## What problem does this actually solve?

Ground that has stopped being usable for what it was built for.

A resident Canada goose flock on managed turf is not a wildlife problem in the abstract. It
is a grounds problem with a cost attached. Droppings across walkways, entrances, lawns and
sports surfaces create a cleaning burden and a slip exposure. Turf gets grazed down and
fouled. Water features become a focal point rather than an amenity. On golf courses it is
playability and presentation. On corporate campuses it is the frontage people walk through
to get to the door. On school and municipal fields it is whether the surface can be used by
children at all.

The awkward part is that the birds are behaving entirely rationally. Mown grass beside open
water with clear sightlines is close to an ideal habitat: food, visibility of approaching
threats, and an escape route. A well-maintained landscape is not incidentally attractive to
geese, it is attractive to them because it is well maintained.

That is why the interventions most sites try first do not hold. Plastic owls, reflective
tape, noise devices and swan decoys all work on the first day and stop working by the
second week, because none of them change the bird's assessment of risk once nothing follows
the stimulus. A dog does, because a dog is an actual predator and the assessment it triggers
is not a guess.

## How does handler-directed hazing work?

The dog changes the site's meaning, and the schedule is what makes the change stick.

A working dog is directed onto the site to move the birds off the ground and off the water.
The intent is never to catch anything. It is to make the location register, in the birds'
own risk calculation, as ground that is patrolled by something that hunts. Geese are good at
that calculation. It is what keeps them alive.

The pattern matters more than the intensity. Visits that are varied in timing and route
prevent the flock from learning a schedule and simply working around it. A site worked at
the same hour every Tuesday teaches geese that Tuesday at that hour is worth avoiding and the
rest of the week is not. A programme that is deliberately irregular teaches them the whole
site is unreliable.

Three things are absolute and they are worth reading before the capability claims:

- **No contact.** The dog does not catch, grip or strike a bird. A dog that will not break
  off on command is not a working dog, and a handler who cannot call one off has no business
  on your property.
- **No injury.** If a bird is hurt, the work has failed on its own terms, not merely on
  legal ones.
- **No nests, no eggs.** They are not touched, moved or interfered with in any way.

## What does the law actually permit?

More than people fear, with sharper edges than most vendors admit.

Canada geese are federally protected under the **Migratory Bird Treaty Act**. The federal
definition of "take" at **50 CFR 10.12** covers pursuing, hunting, shooting, wounding,
killing, trapping, capturing or collecting, and attempts at any of those. The word *pursue*
sits in that list, which is why the way this work is conducted and documented is a legal
question and not a presentational one. You will occasionally see a vendor claim that moving
birds along is categorically outside the definition. That framing is not one we will repeat,
because the definition does not read that way.

**WDFW's Living with Wildlife guidance** on Canada geese is direct about the method: when
directed by a handler, dogs are the method of choice for large open areas such as golf
courses, airports, parks, agricultural fields and corporate parks. It is equally direct that
dogs may be inappropriate in residential areas, in continuously used parks, near roadways
and on large water bodies. We give the second sentence the same weight as the first, which
is why some enquiries end with us saying this is the wrong tool for your site.

**RCW 77.15.240**, the state provision on unlawful use of dogs, covers deer, elk, moose,
caribou, mountain sheep and endangered species. It does not restrict goose hazing.

**50 CFR 21.162**, the Resident Canada Goose Nest and Egg Depredation Order, is the one
most often misdescribed to buyers. Registration under it belongs to landowners, homeowner
associations and local governments, and to their agents. It does not belong to a vendor. A
company cannot hold that registration on your behalf as a service line. Guardian does not
currently offer agent work under a client's registration, and if that changes it will be
stated here rather than sold quietly.

The full legal picture with primary sources is at
[Canada goose law in Washington](/compliance/canada-goose-law-washington/).

## Why do we pause during the molt?

Because for several weeks a year the birds physically cannot leave.

Canada geese undergo a flightless moult during which they shed and regrow their flight
feathers. A bird that cannot fly cannot relocate in response to pressure. Hazing during that
window does not move a flock anywhere. It corrals birds that have no option, which is
neither humane nor defensible, and it is precisely the sort of thing that ends up in a
complaint, a local news item or a board minute that nobody wants to have written.

So we pause. It is published operating policy rather than a case-by-case judgement made on
the day, and it means a programme has a shape across the year rather than being uniform.
Any vendor willing to work your site through the moult is telling you something useful about
how it will handle the next judgement call.

The practical consequence for a grounds manager is that the moult is the period to do the
habitat work instead. That is the right time to look at what the site is offering the birds
in the first place.

## What else changes the outcome besides the dog?

The landscape, and it is worth being honest that we do not control most of it.

A programme run against a site that is otherwise ideal goose habitat has to work harder and
longer than one run alongside sensible grounds decisions. The things that genuinely move
the needle are all in the landscape architecture: interrupted sightlines between turf and
water, planted buffer strips at the water's edge that geese dislike walking through,
reduced areas of continuous mown grass right up to a shoreline, and eliminating deliberate
or accidental feeding.

Feeding is the one worth pressing on. A single well-meaning resident with a bag of bread can
substantially undo a hazing programme, because they are supplying exactly the reward that
outweighs the perceived risk. If a community cannot get agreement on stopping feeding, that
should be said out loud during scoping rather than discovered three months into a contract
that is not performing.

We will tell you where your site is working against you. We are not landscape architects and
we do not design the buffer planting, but we will describe what we see, and we would rather
you spend money on a change that reduces the number of visits you need than on more visits.

## What sites is this suited to?

Large, open, managed ground with a responsible party who can commit to a schedule.

Golf courses are the archetype. Corporate campuses and business parks with lawns and
retention ponds are close behind. HOA common areas, particularly around stormwater ponds and
lake frontage, are among the most common enquiries we get. Municipal and school playing
fields, cemeteries, and the grounds of larger institutional properties all fit. These are the
categories WDFW's own guidance identifies as suited to handler-directed dogs.

Sites that are a poor fit include tightly enclosed residential courtyards, parks in
continuous public use where a working dog cannot be given clear ground, frontages against
busy roads where moving birds creates a traffic hazard, and very large open water where
birds simply relocate a short distance and wait.

There is also a governance question that decides more enquiries than the geography does.
Public parkland requires the land manager to engage us. A vendor cannot begin hazing ground
it does not control, and an adjacent property owner cannot authorise work on land that is
not theirs. Where a site straddles ownership boundaries, that has to be resolved before a
programme starts rather than during it.

## What does a programme look like in practice?

Assessment, an intensive opening period, then a maintenance cadence, with a record at every
stage.

The **assessment** looks at where the birds are loafing, feeding and entering the water,
what the site is offering them, where a dog can and cannot be worked safely, and who
controls each parcel of the ground. It also establishes what success would look like in
terms you can actually check, which is usually about usability of specific areas rather than
a bird count.

The **opening period** is deliberately more frequent. The objective is to break an
established routine, and an established routine does not break on one visit a fortnight.

The **maintenance cadence** steps down to whatever holds the result. This varies by site far
more than vendors like to admit, which is why we would rather review it against the record
than promise a number at the outset.

Throughout, each visit produces a dated record: when we were there, which areas were worked,
what was observed, and what changed. For an HOA board or a public grounds manager, that log
is frequently the real deliverable. It is what allows a body to demonstrate that it responded
to a nuisance proportionately, with a non-lethal method, on advice, rather than either
ignoring the problem or reaching for something it could not defend at a meeting.

## How does this compare with the other things sites try?

Most alternatives fail for the same reason, and it is worth understanding why before
spending on another one.

**Visual scare devices** — plastic owls, coyote silhouettes, reflective tape, balloons,
flags. These work for a few days and then stop, because geese habituate quickly to a
stimulus with no consequence attached. A predator that never moves is not a predator, and
birds establish that within a week or two. Rotating and relocating them extends the effect
somewhat and does not change the arithmetic.

**Noise devices** — propane cannons, distress calls, sonic units. Habituation is the same
problem, with the added difficulty that anything loud enough to matter is loud enough to
generate complaints on a campus, near housing or beside a road. On many of the sites that
call us, noise is not usable at all.

**Physical exclusion** — fencing and grid lines over water. This genuinely works where it
can be installed, and on a small pond it is often a better answer than a hazing programme.
The limits are cost, appearance and scale. It does not suit a golf course or a large campus
lawn, and boards tend to dislike how it looks.

**Habitat modification** — buffer planting at the shoreline, breaking sightlines, reducing
continuous mown turf up to the water. This is the most durable intervention available and
the slowest to take effect. It is also not something we sell, which is precisely why we
recommend it freely.

**Chemical turf treatments.** Not our field, and not something we will advise on. Anything
applied to turf is a licensed activity we do not hold a licence for.

The reason a handler-directed dog holds up where scare devices do not is that habituation
never fully sets in. The stimulus is a live predator with unpredictable timing and a genuine
capability, which is exactly the thing a goose's risk assessment is built to take seriously.
That is also why the programme has to continue. Withdraw it and the assessment updates in
the other direction over the following weeks.

The honest summary is that a dog programme is usually the most effective option for large
open managed ground, exclusion is usually better for a small enclosed water feature, and
habitat modification makes whichever you choose work harder. A vendor who tells you their
method is the answer for every site is describing their inventory rather than your problem.

## What does a visit look like on the day?

Quiet, quick and unremarkable to anybody watching, which is the intention.

We arrive, check the site for anything that has changed since the last visit, and confirm
where people are before the dog comes out. On a golf course that means knowing which holes
are in play. On a campus it means knowing where the lunchtime foot traffic is. The dog is
then worked through the affected ground in a pattern chosen for that day rather than a fixed
route, and the birds lift and move off.

It usually takes far less time than people expect. The work is not a spectacle and it should
not look like one. When residents or staff do ask, the answer we give is the same one on this
page: the dog moves the birds along, nothing is caught, nothing is hurt, and no nests or eggs
are touched.

Then it is written down. Date, areas worked, what was observed, what changed.

## What we will not tell you

That the geese will be gone permanently after a set number of visits.

They will not, and nobody can honestly promise it. Geese are long-lived, site-faithful and
perfectly capable of returning to ground that stops being defended. What a sustained
programme achieves is that your site stops being the easiest option in the area for as long
as the programme runs. That is a real, valuable and maintainable outcome. It is not
eradication, it is not permanent, and describing it as either would be the kind of claim
this company exists not to make.

We also will not quote you a guaranteed result. Where the word guarantee is used anywhere on
this site it is a defined term with written conditions, set out at
[our guarantee](/our-guarantee/), and it covers our process rather than an outcome that
depends on wildlife, weather, neighbouring land and whether somebody keeps feeding them.

If you want this scoped, tell us the acreage, what water is on or beside the site, who owns
each part of the ground, and which areas have actually stopped being usable. We will tell you
whether a dog is the right answer, and if it is not, we will say so.
