## What does the statute actually require?

Two days for an inspection, one day to show, and no consent needed in an emergency.

**RCW 59.18.150** governs a landlord's right of entry. The operative requirement for the
work described on this site is that the landlord "shall give the tenant at least two days'
written notice of his or her intent to enter" for the purposes of inspecting the premises,
making necessary or agreed repairs, alterations or improvements, or supplying necessary or
agreed services.

A shorter period applies to viewings. The statute provides that the tenant "shall not
unreasonably withhold consent to the landlord to enter the dwelling unit at a specified time
where the landlord has given at least one day's notice" of intent to enter to exhibit the
unit to prospective or actual purchasers or tenants.

And there is an exception that swallows the notice requirement entirely in narrow
circumstances: "The landlord may enter the dwelling unit without consent of the tenant in
case of emergency or abandonment."

The statute also places a duty on the tenant, which is frequently overlooked in these
conversations. The tenant shall not unreasonably withhold consent to entry for the listed
purposes, which include inspecting the premises.

## Why is a bed bug inspection not an emergency?

Because urgent and emergency are not the same word, and the distinction is not a technicality.

The emergency exception exists for situations where waiting two days causes damage or
danger: a burst pipe, a fire, a gas smell, a report that a resident is in difficulty. A bed
bug report is genuinely urgent in the ordinary sense. It is not an emergency in the
statutory sense, and relying on the exception to skip notice would be a poor decision made
under pressure.

The practical consequence is straightforward. Plan on the two days. A manager who receives a
report on Monday and wants an inspector inside on Tuesday is planning a problem, not an
inspection, and the tenant who is served defective notice now has a second grievance to raise
alongside the first.

The related mistake is treating the two days as the whole of the lead time. It is a
statutory floor, not a schedule. In practice you also need time to identify the units, draft
the notice, get it served, and be reachable when residents call with questions about it.

## What counts as written notice?

Written, delivered, and recorded. The statute specifies written notice of intent to enter,
and the rest is a documentation discipline rather than a legal subtlety.

Whatever method you use, use it consistently and keep evidence of it. The question that
arises later is never "did the law require notice." It is "can you show what was served, to
whom, and when." A notice process that cannot answer that produces exactly the same outcome
as no notice at all when it is challenged.

Three things worth building into the notice itself, none of which the statute requires but
all of which reduce friction on the day:

**What the visit involves.** A resident who reads "inspection" imagines something different
from what a handler and a dog actually do. A sentence explaining that a trained dog will
walk through the rooms, that no chemicals are applied and that nothing is treated removes
most of the anxiety and most of the phone calls.

**What the resident does not need to do.** People instinctively deep clean, launder bedding,
strip the bed and move furniture before an inspection. All of that makes the search worse.
Saying so in the notice is the single highest-value sentence you can add to it.

**Who to contact with a question or a scheduling conflict.** A named person, reachable
before the date. Residents who cannot get an answer to a reasonable question are residents
who do not open the door.

## Why is notice the binding constraint on building-wide work?

Because it is the only part of the process that cannot be compressed, and it fails at scale
in a way it never does for a single unit.

For one unit, notice is routine administration. For forty or four hundred units it becomes a
logistics exercise with a hard deadline attached, and it is the step that most often derails
a schedule.

The failure mode is consistent. A manager books an inspection for the following week.
Notices go out slightly late, or to a stale resident list, or to unit numbers that do not
match the current occupancy. On the day, a substantial share of doors do not open. What
comes out the other end is not a building sweep. It is a partial search producing a document
that looks comprehensive and is not, which is worse than having done nothing, because now
there is a report understating its own gaps.

That is why we book building-wide work far enough out for the notice process to be done
properly rather than hurriedly, and why we ask about your access plan before we ask about
your preferred date. The sequencing matters: access first, calendar second.

## How do you run notice at scale without it falling over?

Six things, learned from watching where it goes wrong.

**Start from the current rent roll, not last quarter's.** Unit lists drift. A notice served
to a unit whose occupant changed six weeks ago is a notice that did not reach anybody.

**Serve in blocks that match how the work will actually run.** If a building will be swept
over two days, the notices should reflect which units fall on which day. A single blanket
notice for a multi-day sweep creates confusion about when anyone should expect a knock.

**Build in a buffer beyond the statutory minimum.** Two days is the floor. Serving on a
Friday for a Monday visit technically clears it and leaves no room for a resident to raise a
conflict.

**Name a contact who is actually available.** Not a general mailbox.

**Handle language needs deliberately.** In agricultural workforce housing across Whatcom and
Skagit, and in a good deal of urban multifamily, a notice in English only will not
communicate to every household. This is a practical access issue before it is anything else.

**Track what was served and keep it.** The notice file and the inspection report belong
together. Six months later they answer two halves of the same question.

## What happens to the units that still do not open?

They are recorded, by unit number, as not searched.

This is the point where a documentation standard either exists or does not. A unit that
could not be entered is not a unit that is clear, and writing it down as clear is the single
most damaging thing an inspection provider can do to a client. Our reports separate three
outcomes — confirmed, not confirmed and inconclusive — and a unit that was never entered
appears in the report as an explicit gap rather than as an absence.

That is uncomfortable to read on the day and it is the reason the document survives being
read by somebody hostile two years later. A report listing forty-four results for a
fifty-unit building, without mentioning the six, invites a reader to conclude the building
was screened. It was not.

The standard is set out at
[what makes a report defensible](/compliance/what-a-defensible-report-contains/), and the
practical consequence is that a good notice process directly improves the quality of the
evidence you end up holding. Notice is not administrative overhead surrounding the
inspection. It is part of the inspection's value.

## Does this work differently in particular settings?

The statute reads the same. The operational reality varies enough to change the planning.

**Student housing.** Turnover compresses into two weeks twice a year, and during those
windows units may be empty, which removes the notice problem entirely and is exactly why
turnover is the cheapest moment to screen. Outside those windows, residence life processes
usually sit alongside the statutory requirement rather than replacing it.

**Senior living and care settings.** Residents may need more than the minimum notice and
more explanation, and family members frequently want to be present. Building the
communication plan around that is not a courtesy, it is what makes the access rate workable.

**Agricultural workforce housing.** Common across the northern counties, and the
organisation controlling access is frequently not the property owner. Establish who is
authorised to admit an inspector before anything is scheduled, because the answer is often
not what the conventional multifamily assumption suggests.

**Short-stay lodging.** Not landlord-tenant law at all. A hotel room between guests is
scheduled around housekeeping and occupancy rather than statutory notice, which is why
lodging sweeps are usually easier to arrange and much harder to fit into a revenue calendar.

**Vacant units.** No occupant, no notice question, full access. This is why turnover
screening is operationally the easiest work in this field and why we push it as a routine
rather than a response.

## What if a tenant refuses after proper notice?

The statute anticipates it, and the answer is narrower than either side usually assumes.

**RCW 59.18.150** provides that a tenant shall not unreasonably withhold consent to the
landlord entering to inspect the premises, make necessary or agreed repairs, alterations or
improvements, supply necessary or agreed services, or exhibit the unit to prospective or
actual purchasers, mortgagees, tenants, workers or contractors.

The operative word is *unreasonably*. A tenant raising a genuine scheduling conflict, asking
for a different day, requesting that someone be present, or asking what the visit involves
is not refusing. They are negotiating the specified time, which the statute contemplates. A
manager who treats every question as obstruction will generate the obstruction they expected.

A flat refusal after proper notice, with no reason offered and no alternative accepted, is a
different situation, and it is one worth handling deliberately rather than by escalation on
the doorstep. Document what notice was served and when. Document the refusal. Offer an
alternative in writing. Then take advice, because forcing entry over an objection is a
decision with consequences beyond the inspection.

In practice, most refusals in this specific context are not about access at all. They are
about fear: of blame, of a chargeback, of being told to discard belongings, of the visit
being visible to neighbours. A notice that explains the visit is short, involves no
chemicals, treats nothing, and is not an accusation removes most of that. Buildings with a
reputation for blaming residents have measurably worse access rates, and the cost lands on
the owner.

## What about vacant units, common areas and storage?

Different rules, and the difference is worth exploiting.

**Vacant units** have no occupant and therefore no notice question. Full access, no
scheduling negotiation, no privacy consideration, and no clutter obstructing the search.
This is why turnover is operationally the easiest and cheapest moment to screen anything,
and why we push it as a routine rather than as a response to a problem.

**Common areas** — lobbies, corridors, laundry rooms, shared lounges, management offices —
are under the landlord's control and are not dwelling units. They also matter more than
people expect. Upholstered furniture in a shared lounge, and laundry rooms where belongings
from many units pass through, are exactly the kind of shared surfaces worth including in a
building sweep.

**Resident storage areas** sit somewhere in between and depend on how the lease treats them.
Worth establishing before the day rather than in the corridor.

The planning consequence is that a building sweep should be designed in layers: common areas
and vacant units, which need no notice, plus occupied units, which set the lead time. Where
speed matters, the first layer can often run while notices for the second are still running
their two days.

## What does a fifty-unit sweep actually look like on a calendar?

Working backwards from the visit, which is the only direction that works.

Start with the date you want the search to happen. Notices must be served at least two days
before, which is a floor rather than a plan, so allow more. Before you can serve them you
need a current unit list from the rent roll rather than a stale one, the notices drafted with
a named contact on them, and a decision about which units fall on which day if the work spans
more than one.

Before any of that, you need the access plan: who holds keys, which units have restricted
access, which residents need particular handling, whether anyone needs the notice in another
language, and who is authorised to admit an inspector to each space.

That is why we ask for the access plan before the calendar. A manager who calls on Monday
wanting a fifty-unit sweep on Thursday is not describing a schedule, and the honest response
is to say so and book a date that will actually produce a complete document.

Two further practical points. Build in the units that will not open regardless — there are
always some — and decide in advance whether a second pass for those is worth arranging, or
whether they will simply be recorded as not searched. And expect the first sweep of a
building to run slower than the estimate, because nobody knows the access quirks yet.

## How does this connect to everything else?

Notice determines coverage. Coverage determines what the report can honestly say. What the
report can honestly say determines whether it is worth anything when somebody challenges it.

The chain runs in that order, and it is why a page about a notice statute belongs on a
detection company's website at all. A property manager who treats notice as paperwork gets a
weaker document at the end. One who treats it as the first step of the inspection gets a
complete one.

There is a quieter benefit as well. A notice process run properly tends to raise the access
rate on every subsequent visit to the same building, because residents learn that these
visits are short, undramatic, involve no chemicals and carry no accusation. Access improves
with reputation, and reputation is built by the second and third visit rather than the
first. Buildings we have worked repeatedly are materially easier to screen completely than
buildings we are visiting for the first time, and the difference shows up directly in how
many units end up recorded as inconclusive.
