## What protects Canada geese?

Federal law, primarily, and the protection is broader than most property managers expect.

Canada geese are migratory birds covered by the **Migratory Bird Treaty Act**. That is the
starting point for every question on this page, and it is why goose management is not
analogous to dealing with a nuisance that carries no protection at all.

The definition that does the work sits at **50 CFR 10.12**, where take means

> to pursue, hunt, shoot, wound, kill, trap, capture, or collect, or attempt to pursue,
> hunt, shoot, wound, kill, trap, capture, or collect.

Read the first verb. **Pursue** is inside the definition of take, and attempts are included
alongside completed acts.

You will encounter vendors and trade materials asserting confidently that harassment or
hazing is categorically not take. We will not repeat that framing, because the definition
does not read that way and because a company willing to reassure you with a convenient
reading of a federal regulation is a company that will be convenient about other things
later.

What we will tell you is what our work actually involves, which is a narrower and more useful
statement: handler-directed movement of birds off ground, with no contact, no injury, and no
nests or eggs disturbed.

## What does WDFW say about using dogs?

That they are the method of choice for large open areas, and inappropriate in several
settings people often want to use them in.

Washington Department of Fish and Wildlife's *Living with Wildlife* guidance on Canada geese
is direct:

> When directed by a handler, dogs are the method of choice for large open areas such as
> golf courses, airports, parks, agricultural fields, and corporate parks.

That is an unusually clear endorsement of a specific method from a state wildlife agency,
and it is the reason this service exists in the form it does.

The same guidance is equally clear about limits. WDFW notes that dogs may be inappropriate
in residential areas, in continuously used parks, near roadways, and on large water bodies.
Those are not footnotes. A dog worked beside a busy road can push birds into traffic. A dog
worked in a park in continuous public use cannot be given clear ground and will generate
complaints. On a large water body the birds simply move a short distance and wait.

We apply both halves. Some enquiries end with us saying a dog is the wrong tool for that
site, which is a conversation that costs us the job and saves the client a contract that
would not have performed.

## Who may do nest and egg work?

The landowner, the association or the local government. Not a vendor.

The **Resident Canada Goose Nest and Egg Depredation Order** at **50 CFR 21.162** is the
federal mechanism permitting registered parties to destroy nests and eggs of resident Canada
geese without an individual permit. The authorised methods include egg oiling using 100 per
cent corn oil, and nest and egg destruction. Registrants are required to use non-lethal
techniques to the extent they minimise take.

The critical structural point, and the one most often misrepresented to buyers, is **who
registers**. Registration belongs to landowners, homeowner associations and local
governments, and to their agents. It does not belong to a pest or wildlife vendor as a
standalone service line. A company cannot hold that registration on your behalf and sell you
nest work under it.

An operator could act as an agent under a client's own registration. That is a real and
lawful arrangement, and it is a materially different thing from a vendor offering nest
removal as a menu item.

**Guardian does not offer nest or egg work.** We do not currently act as an agent under a
client's registration either. If that ever changes it will be stated plainly here rather
than quietly added to a quote. If another company offers to handle your nests without ever
mentioning that you are the party who must be registered, that omission tells you something
about how they will handle the next question.

## What does state law add?

Less than people assume, and one commonly cited provision does not apply at all.

**RCW 77.15.240** addresses unlawful use of dogs. It is frequently raised in conversations
about hazing, usually by someone who has read the title rather than the text. The provision
covers deer, elk, moose, caribou, mountain sheep and endangered species. **It does not
restrict goose hazing.**

What state and local rules do affect is the practical envelope around the work rather than
the method itself. Local leash and park ordinances apply to a working dog like any other
dog, and they differ by jurisdiction. Commercial activity on WDFW-managed land is subject to
that agency's own permitting. Municipal parks departments set their own conditions for
vendors operating on their ground.

None of that is exotic. It just means the question "may we do this here" is usually a
question about the specific parcel and its manager, not a question about hazing in general.

## Why does the flightless molt stop the work?

Because for several weeks a year the birds physically cannot leave, which removes the entire
mechanism.

Canada geese undergo a flightless moult while they shed and regrow flight feathers. During
that period a bird cannot relocate in response to pressure. Hazing at that point does not
move a flock somewhere else. It corrals birds that have no option, which is neither humane
nor defensible, and it is the kind of thing that ends up in a complaint or a local news item.

Guardian pauses hazing during the molt. That is **published operating policy**, not a
judgement made on the day by whoever is on site, and stating it in advance is deliberate. A
vendor willing to work your ground through the molt is telling you how they will handle the
next judgement call too.

For a grounds manager the useful consequence is that the molt is the window for the other
half of the work: habitat modification. Buffer planting at the water's edge, interrupted
sightlines between turf and water, and reducing continuous mown grass running to a shoreline
are the durable interventions, and none of them depend on a dog.

## Snow geese are a different question

Worth its own section, because in the Skagit Valley the confusion is constant.

Skagit is famous for enormous wintering flocks, and those are **snow geese** from the
Wrangel Island population, arriving in counts frequently exceeding fifty thousand across the
valley and the Skagit Wildlife Area, which WDFW manages across roughly 18,138 acres in
twenty-one units. The agricultural conflict around them is real and long-running, driven
partly by shifts in what the valley grows, and WDFW's principal management response has been
to encourage hunting, including a quality hunt programme run in partnership with local
farmers and funded through hunting licence revenue and a federal public-access programme.

That is a different bird, a different population, a different management framework and a
different set of sites from the resident Canada geese on a corporate lawn or an HOA
retention pond.

Guardian's goose service is resident Canada goose deterrent work on managed grounds. We do
not offer snow goose work on agricultural land. If a vendor offers to send a dog at your snow
geese on a Skagit field, ask a great many questions before agreeing.

## What other non-lethal methods exist, and why do they fail?

Habituation, mostly. It is the single concept that explains almost every disappointed
grounds budget in this field.

**Visual scare devices.** Plastic owls, coyote silhouettes, reflective tape, flags,
balloons, swan decoys. These work for days rather than seasons. A predator that never moves
and never follows through stops being a predator in the birds' assessment within a week or
two. Rotating and relocating them extends the effect somewhat without changing the
underlying problem.

**Noise devices.** Propane cannons, distress calls, sonic units. Same habituation curve,
plus a practical constraint: anything loud enough to be effective is loud enough to generate
complaints on a campus, near housing or beside a road. On many sites that call us, noise is
not usable at all.

**Physical exclusion.** Fencing and grid lines over water genuinely work where they can be
installed, and on a small enclosed pond exclusion is often a better answer than a hazing
programme. The limits are cost, appearance and scale. It does not suit a golf course or a
large open campus lawn.

**Habitat modification.** Buffer planting at the shoreline, breaking sightlines between turf
and water, reducing continuous mown grass running to the water's edge, and eliminating
feeding. This is the most durable intervention available and the slowest to show results. It
is also the one no vendor profits from, which is why you should weigh a recommendation for
it more heavily than a recommendation for anything else.

**Feeding.** Worth naming separately. A single well-meaning person with a bag of bread can
substantially undo a hazing programme, because they are supplying a reward that outweighs
the perceived risk. If a community cannot reach agreement on stopping feeding, that should
be said during scoping rather than discovered three months into a contract that is not
performing.

A handler-directed dog holds up where scare devices do not because habituation never fully
sets in: the stimulus is a live predator with unpredictable timing and genuine capability.
That is also precisely why the programme has to continue. Withdraw it and the birds' risk
assessment updates in the other direction over the following weeks.

## Why does the droppings problem carry legal weight of its own?

Because it moves the issue from amenity to liability, which changes who has to care.

Goose droppings on walkways, entrances, plazas and sports surfaces create a slip exposure
across ground that staff, residents, visitors and members cross daily. For a property owner
or an association, that is a maintenance obligation and a risk-management question rather
than a landscaping preference, and it is usually what converts a long-running annoyance into
a funded project.

The relevant point for this page is what that means for documentation. Where a body is later
asked whether it responded reasonably to a known hazard, the useful answer is a dated record:
that the problem was assessed, that a non-lethal method consistent with agency guidance was
selected, that it was carried out on a schedule, and that the operating limits were
observed. The absence of such a record is the weak point, not the presence of geese.

This is the same logic that governs inspection reporting elsewhere on this site. A programme
that produces no record produces no protection.

## What happens during a site assessment?

Four questions, answered on the ground before anything is quoted.

**Where are the birds actually spending time?** Loafing areas, feeding areas and water access
points are usually distinct, and a programme that works only one of them pushes birds around
the site rather than off it.

**What is the site offering them?** Sightlines, continuous mown turf to a water edge, absence
of buffer planting, and any feeding. This is where the durable improvements are identified,
and none of them involve us.

**Where can a dog be worked safely?** Roads, public access, enclosed areas and large open
water all constrain the method, and WDFW's own guidance flags several of them.

**Who controls each parcel?** Sites frequently straddle ownership boundaries, and a vendor
cannot work ground its client does not control. Where a public parks department, a
neighbouring association or a sovereign tribal government holds part of the ground, that has
to be resolved before a programme starts rather than during it.

The assessment also establishes what success would look like in terms somebody can actually
check, which is normally about the usability of specific areas rather than a bird count.
Counts fluctuate for reasons that have nothing to do with the programme.

## What should a board or grounds manager actually do?

Five steps, in order.

**Establish who controls the ground.** Public parkland requires the parks department or land
manager to engage the vendor. An adjacent association cannot authorise work on land it does
not own, however much the flock is genuinely its problem.

**Decide whether you want to register** under 50 CFR 21.162. That is your decision as
landowner, association or local government, and it is separate from hiring anyone.

**Ask the vendor what they will not do.** The answer should include nests, eggs, contact,
injury and the molt period. A vendor with no limits to describe has not thought about it.

**Get the work recorded.** For a board the visit log is frequently the real deliverable: it
is what demonstrates the body responded to a nuisance proportionately, non-lethally and on
advice, rather than doing nothing or reaching for something indefensible. What that record
should contain is at
[what makes a report defensible](/compliance/what-a-defensible-report-contains/).

**Plan for a programme, not a visit.** Geese return to ground that stops being defended, and
any vendor promising permanent removal after a fixed number of visits is describing a sale
rather than an outcome.
