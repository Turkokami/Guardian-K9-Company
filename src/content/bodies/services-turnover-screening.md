## Why is an empty unit the best inspection you will ever buy?

Because every constraint that makes occupied inspection difficult is simply absent.

Think about what actually slows a unit inspection. Serving entry notice and waiting the
statutory period. Negotiating a time with somebody who works shifts. Securing a pet.
Working around furniture, stored boxes, and belongings under and around the bed. Managing a
resident who is anxious about being blamed. Recording half a room as inconclusive because it
could not be reached.

None of that exists in a vacant unit. There is no occupant, so **RCW 59.18.150** entry
notice does not arise. There is no schedule to negotiate. There is nothing in the way. The
search is faster, more complete, and produces fewer inconclusive entries than the same unit
would with somebody living in it.

That combination — lowest cost per unit, highest coverage per unit — is why turnover
screening is the single most under-used service in this field, and why the operators who
adopt it tend to stop having the expensive conversations that everyone else keeps having.

## What is the baseline for?

Dating the condition of the unit at handover, which is the fact everything later turns on.

Consider the common dispute. Four months into a tenancy a resident reports bites, an
inspection confirms bed bugs, and the question becomes who pays. Under
**RCW 59.18.060(4)** the duty sits with the landlord except in a single-family residence
and except where the tenant caused the infestation, and **RCW 59.18.130** is the route to
tenant liability where causation is established.

Without a baseline, that conversation is two recollections in conflict. The manager believes
the unit was fine at handover. The tenant believes it was not. Neither has anything, and the
party asserting the exception is the party who needs to make it stand up.

With a baseline, one side of the timeline is documented: this unit was searched on this
date by a named handler, and here is what was found, including nothing. That does not decide
the question. Causation depends on facts nobody inspected — travel, visitors, second-hand
furniture, movement from an adjoining unit — and no dog establishes origin. What it does is
convert a symmetric argument into an asymmetric one, and it does so at the cheapest possible
moment.

## Move-out or move-in?

Usually the same visit, because the useful window is the gap between them.

The distinction matters more when the vacancy is long. **Move-out screening** tells you what
the departing tenancy left behind, which is information about the unit you have just got
back and about whether remediation is needed before you re-let. **Move-in screening** dates
the condition at handover, which is the evidentiary function described above.

When a unit turns quickly, one search covers both purposes: it is the last word on the old
tenancy and the first word on the new one. When a unit sits vacant for months and is
refurbished in between, there is an argument for screening closer to the handover date so
the baseline reflects what the incoming tenant actually receives.

Where a unit is being screened after cleaning and painting, tell us. Cleaning removes some of
the physical evidence a handler would otherwise confirm at an alert location. It does not
make the search pointless — the dog is indicating on odour rather than on visible staining —
but it changes what visual confirmation can establish, and that belongs in the record.

## Where does turnover screening pay for itself fastest?

Wherever turnover is high, compressed, or arriving from many different places.

**Student housing.** Move-in and move-out compress an entire year of turnover into two weeks,
twice. Belongings arrive from every market a student came from, which makes origin tracing
meaningless and screening the only sensible response. The units are also genuinely empty
during the window, which is the ideal condition. The scheduling problem is that everybody
wants the same fortnight, so this has to be booked months out.

**Workforce and high-mobility housing.** Kent Valley distribution housing, Everett's
shift-pattern workforce stock, and the contractor-adjacent rental market around Cherry Point
in Ferndale all turn over faster than conventional leases. Higher turnover means more
screening opportunities and a higher value per screening.

**Corporate and relocation housing.** Bellevue in particular has short-stay corporate units
that turn over far faster than a normal lease. That is a cadence problem rather than an
inspection problem, and turnover screening is the natural shape of the answer.

**Military-adjacent housing.** Oak Harbor turns on posting orders rather than the calendar,
and military households move with goods packed, shipped and stored by third parties, which
is a documented introduction pathway that the household has no control over.

**Senior living.** New residents frequently arrive with furniture from a home of many years,
and residents cannot always report reliably afterwards. Screening at intake replaces
complaint-driven discovery with something that actually works in that setting.

## What about the furniture that comes with the tenant?

That is the other half of the problem, and it is worth treating separately.

Turnover screening establishes the state of the empty unit. It says nothing about what
arrives the following week. For settings where incoming furniture is a known pathway —
second-hand purchases, donated furnishings, storage that has been in a facility for months,
or households consolidating after a displacement — screening items before they enter is a
distinct and cheaper intervention.

That has been an unusually live issue in the northern counties. After the November 2021
Nooksack flooding, where more than 85% of structures in Sumas were damaged and hundreds of
people across the north county were displaced, replacement and donated furniture moved
through the region at volume. Displacement, temporary housing and replaced furnishings are
all recognised introduction pathways, and they arrive on a schedule nobody plans.

## How do you build a programme rather than a habit?

Decide the rule in advance, write it down, and make it survive staff turnover.

The common failure is not that operators disagree with turnover screening. It is that they
do it when somebody remembers, which means it happens after an incident and stops six months
later when the person who cared moves on. A programme is a rule applied without a decision
being made each time.

Three workable rules, in ascending cost:

**Screen every turn.** Simplest to administer, highest coverage, best baseline data. Sensible
in buildings with any history, in student housing, and in high-turnover workforce stock.

**Screen a rotating share of turns.** Cheaper, and still produces a usable picture of the
building over a year. Reasonable where there is no known history and the budget will not
carry every unit. The important part is that the rotation is genuinely systematic rather
than "whichever ones we get around to," because a self-selected sample tells you very little.

**Screen every turn in flagged buildings, and a share elsewhere.** What most multi-property
operators land on. It concentrates spend where the history justifies it.

Whichever rule you pick, the value compounds. One screened unit is a data point. Two years of
screened turns is a record of the building, and a record is what answers the question about
what you knew and when.

## What does the search involve in an empty unit?

The same discipline as any other search, run faster because nothing is in the way.

The dog works the perimeter of each room first, then whatever furniture remains, then the
specific assemblies that matter: where skirting meets flooring, around window and door
casings, and any built-in furniture, closet framing or fitted seating. In a fully cleared
unit those junctions and voids are most of what there is to search, and they are exactly the
places a departing tenant's problem would have persisted.

Two things are genuinely different from an occupied search. There is far less soft furnishing
to work, which removes the highest-probability targets and makes the search quicker but not
less thorough. And there is nothing obstructing access, so the proportion of the unit
recorded as inconclusive drops close to zero. That second point is the quiet advantage:
turnover results are more complete than occupied results, which makes them better baseline
data.

Where the unit retains landlord-supplied furniture — a furnished let, a corporate unit, a
care setting — the search looks much more like an occupied one, because the furniture is the
target. Furnished turnover is therefore slower per unit than unfurnished, and worth flagging
when scoping.

When the dog indicates, the handler examines the location directly before anything is
recorded, looking for live insects, cast skins, eggs or dark faecal staining. In an empty
unit that examination is often easier, because the furniture can be moved without asking
anyone.

## What if the unit was just treated?

Then this is verification, and it is a different exercise with a different meaning.

Where a departing tenancy ended with a confirmed infestation and remediation has been carried
out before re-letting, screening the empty unit is doing two jobs at once: establishing the
handover baseline, and independently checking whether the work achieved what it was meant to.
The second job is the one an operator cannot credibly do for itself.

There is an important caveat. Residual scent sources can persist after a population is gone —
cast skins, old staining, dead insects in voids — which is why a post-treatment search is
genuinely harder to interpret than a first inspection. An alert in a recently treated unit is
not automatically a failure of the treatment, and a report that presents it as one is
overreaching. That is precisely why the three recording states exist and why unconfirmed
alerts stay in the document rather than being resolved in either direction.

Tell us the treatment history before the visit. It does not change what we do; it changes
what the result means, and it belongs in the record either way. The dedicated treatment of
this is at [post-treatment verification](/services/bed-bug-verification-inspection/).

## What do you do with the results across a portfolio?

Aggregate them, because that is where turnover data becomes worth more than the sum of the
visits.

An individual turnover all-clear is a small, boring document. Two years of them across a
portfolio is something else: a record of which buildings produce findings and which do not,
how often, and whether that is changing. That tells you where to concentrate spend, which
buildings justify a full sweep, and which internal processes are working.

Three things worth tracking, none of which requires anything more than the reports you
already hold. The **rate of confirmed findings per hundred turns**, by building, which is the
closest thing to a real risk signal you will get. The **rate of inconclusive results**, which
is a measure of access quality rather than of pests and usually points at an operational
problem. And **repeat findings in the same unit across successive tenancies**, which is the
strongest indicator of a reservoir that unit-level remediation keeps missing.

That third one is the most valuable and the most commonly missed, because it only becomes
visible when somebody looks across time rather than at each incident. A unit that produces a
confirmed finding at three consecutive turnovers is not having bad luck with tenants.

This is also the argument for a single document format across every property, however many
vendors or properties are involved. Twelve buildings with twelve differently structured
reports is a filing cabinet. Twelve buildings on one format and one cadence is a dataset.

## What comes out of it?

The same document format as every other inspection we run, which is deliberate.

A dated, signed record naming the handler who did the work, the unit, what was searched, and
the result. Alerts are recorded by location with a visual confirmation result in one of three
states: confirmed, not confirmed, or inconclusive. Where nothing is found you get a written
all-clear stating what was searched and what was not.

For a vacant unit that all-clear is usually the whole document, and it is the least
interesting page in the file on the day it is issued. It is also the page most likely to
matter later, which is exactly why the format does not change for an empty unit. We retain
the file so it can be reissued, because the request nearly always arrives long after anyone
remembers the visit.

## What are the limits?

Three, and they are the same three that apply to any screening.

It does not prove absence. An all-clear says what was searched on a date and that nothing
was found, not that the unit is free of bed bugs. No method proves a negative.

It does not establish who caused anything. It dates a condition. Causation depends on facts
nobody inspected.

And detection varies between working teams more than the industry admits, which is why we
publish the handler, the dog, the certifying body and the certification dates instead of a
percentage. The figures and the sources are at [detection accuracy](/detection-accuracy/).

If you want this scoped, the useful things to have ready are your typical turn volume, the
number of properties, whether turns cluster seasonally, and whether any building has known
history. With those we can tell you which of the three rules above is worth running and what
it would cost across a year rather than per door. The wider programme structure is at

where we are fastest is at [where we work](/service-area/).

A closing word on why we push this service harder than the others. Turnover screening is the
only work in this field that is genuinely preventive in effect, in the sense that it finds
problems while they are small and cheap rather than after somebody has been bitten for six
weeks. It is also the least dramatic, the easiest to defer, and the first thing cut when a
budget tightens. Those two facts are related, and the operators who hold the line on it are
the ones who spend least on this problem over a decade.

It is worth saying plainly that this is the recommendation least profitable to us in the
short term. A screening programme run properly reduces the number of emergency call-outs,
building-wide sweeps and post-treatment verifications a property needs, and all three of
those are larger invoices than a turnover visit. We make the argument anyway, because a
client who spends less over five years is a client who is still calling in year six.
