## Why Bellingham is a prime work zone rather than a northern detour

Because this is where the schedule lives.

Most canine detection in Washington is organised out of the Puget Sound corridor, and the
north gets served on the end of a route when somebody is already coming that way. That
arrangement produces exactly the behaviour you would expect. Long lead times, a
willingness to promise a date and then move it, and a strong preference for the job that
does not require driving to Whatcom County.

Guardian is arranged the other way round. Whatcom and Skagit are the prime work zones and
Bellingham is the largest rental market in them. Everything south of Everett is a travel
market for us, booked in advance rather than promised same-day. I would rather state that
plainly than claim a statewide same-day capability nobody honestly has.

## What the rental registration programme means for your documentation

More than people expect, and not for the reason you would guess.

Bellingham operates a Rental Registration and Safety Inspection Program under **BMC 6.15**.
Residential rentals must be registered before they are let. A certificate of registration
has to be posted in each unit where a tenant can see it. Units are inspected on roughly a
three to three-and-a-half year cycle, and an owner may use either a City inspector or a
qualified private inspector who has completed the programme's training and holds an
accepted credential. Renting an unregistered unit carries financial penalties.

The programme is not about pests. It is life and fire safety, and it would be wrong to
tell you it obliges anything regarding bed bugs. What it does is shape the local habit.
Owners and managers in this city are already accustomed to a third-party inspector
walking their units on a schedule, to producing a certificate when asked, and to keeping a
compliance file that somebody outside the company might one day read.

That changes how a detection report lands. In markets with no registration culture, a
dated third-party inspection document arrives as an unfamiliar object, and half the
conversation is spent explaining what it is for. Here it drops into an existing filing
habit. When a tenant escalates, or an owner asks what you knew and when, the document is
already in the place where such documents live.

## Who is responsible for bed bugs in a Bellingham rental?

The landlord, under state law, with the two carve-outs that always apply.

**RCW 59.18.060(4)** requires a landlord to provide a reasonable pest control programme at
the start of a tenancy and, other than in a single-family residence, to control
infestation during the tenancy except where the tenant caused it. **RCW 59.18.130** sets
out tenant duties and is the source of tenant liability for extermination costs where the
tenant is the cause.

Bellingham does not add a municipal provision naming bed bugs specifically. Seattle does,
through SMC 22.206.160.A.3 and Director's Rule 2-88, with penalties running from $150 to
$500 per day. That difference catches out regional managers who hold assets in both
cities and write one policy for the portfolio. A Seattle-calibrated policy over-documents
here; a policy written for Whatcom under-documents the Seattle assets.

The practical consequence in Bellingham is that when causation is disputed, there is no
municipal inspection finding to lean on. What you have is your own record. That is an
argument for keeping a good one, not an argument for relaxing.

There is a fuller summary at
[who is responsible under Washington landlord-tenant law](/compliance/washington-landlord-tenant-bed-bugs/),
and the entry rules at
[entry notice for unit inspections](/compliance/entry-notice-for-unit-inspections/).

## How does the university calendar drive the work here?

It compresses a year of turnover risk into a handful of weeks, twice.

Western Washington University sits above a rental market that turns over on the academic
calendar rather than continuously. Leases in the near-campus neighbourhoods cluster around
the same dates, which means move-out and move-in happen across the same short window in a
few thousand beds. Belongings arrive from wherever each student spent the summer, and from
wherever their family lives, which makes origin tracing pointless and screening the only
sensible response.

The operational problem is that everybody wants the same fortnight. A screening programme
that is booked in July for late August works. One that is arranged in the third week of
August does not, because the dog and the handler can only be in one building at a time and
the window does not extend to suit demand.

If you manage student-adjacent property here, the honest advice is to treat the calendar
as fixed infrastructure and book against it months out. There is more on the setting at
[student housing and campuses](/commercial/student-housing/) and on the mechanics at
[move-in and move-out screening](/services/turnover-screening/).

## What does the older near-campus housing stock change about an inspection?

It changes how long a proper search takes, which is the part people underestimate.

A good deal of the rental stock in the older Bellingham neighbourhoods and in Fairhaven is
pre-war housing that has been subdivided. Trim, baseboard, floor junctions, picture rails
and the voids behind them offer far more harbourage than the sealed assemblies of a
2015-built apartment. There are more places to search, the search is slower, and a
scheduling assumption borrowed from newer stock will be wrong.

Newer construction is not lower risk, and I want to be careful not to imply it is. Bed
bugs arrive with occupants, not with the building. What newer construction changes is
where they can go once they arrive and how quickly a search can rule a room out. Both
matter for scoping, neither tells you anything about likelihood.

Clutter matters more than either. A heavily occupied studio can take longer than a large
sparse unit, and a unit that cannot be searched properly is recorded as inconclusive
rather than clear. We would rather write that word down than produce a tidy report that
overstates what we actually saw.

## Does the ferry terminal and cross-border traffic change the risk picture?

It changes the introduction pathways, which is a different thing from changing the odds.

The southern terminus of the Alaska Marine Highway is at Fairhaven, so long-haul passenger
traffic arrives here with baggage that has spent days in shared holds and berths. The
Canadian border is close enough that cross-border visitors are a routine part of local
short-stay lodging in a way they simply are not in south sound markets.

I am not going to attach a number to that. I have not seen a dataset that measures
introduction rates by travel mode in this county, and inventing one to make the point
sound more scientific would be the kind of thing this site exists to avoid. What I will
say is that lodging operators here handle a higher share of guests who have just completed
a long shared-transport journey, and that screening after a season rather than after a
complaint is the cheaper way round.

The lodging-specific detail is at [hotels and lodging](/commercial/hotels-and-lodging/),
and the traveller-facing version at

## What about the geese at Lake Padden and the city parks?

Resident Canada geese on managed turf are exactly the setting where a handler-directed dog
is the recognised tool, and the constraints are worth stating before the capability.

WDFW's Living with Wildlife guidance is direct about the method: when directed by a
handler, dogs are the method of choice for large open areas such as golf courses,
airports, parks, agricultural fields and corporate parks. The same guidance notes that
dogs may be inappropriate in residential areas, in continuously used parks, near roadways
and on large water bodies. We take the second half as seriously as the first, and there
are sites in this city where the honest answer is that a dog is the wrong tool.

What we do is handler-directed hazing: no contact, no injury, no nests or eggs disturbed.
Canada geese are federally protected under the Migratory Bird Treaty Act, and "take" is
defined at 50 CFR 10.12 to include pursuit, which is why the way the work is conducted and
documented matters rather than being a technicality.

Two things we will not do. We do not offer nest or egg work as a Guardian service.
Registration under the Resident Canada Goose Nest and Egg Depredation Order at 50 CFR
21.162 belongs to the landowner, the association or the local government, never to a
vendor. And hazing pauses during the flightless molt, because birds that cannot fly cannot
be moved lawfully or humanely. That pause is published operating policy rather than
something you find out about in July.

Work on public parkland requires the land manager to engage us. A vendor cannot decide to
start hazing ground it does not control, and any company that offers to is telling you
something about how it operates. The legal detail is at
[Canada goose law in Washington](/compliance/canada-goose-law-washington/).

## How good is a detection dog, really?

Better than a visual search in most settings, and far more variable than the marketing
suggests.

The high accuracy figure that circulates in this industry comes from controlled
laboratory conditions with planted hides. The field research, run in naturally infested
apartments, found substantially lower average detection and an enormous spread between
teams. The figures and the citations are on our [detection accuracy](/detection-accuracy/)
page, where they belong, rather than being reshaped into a sales line here.

The spread is the honest headline. It means the variable is the team, not the species. A
detection dog is not a specification you buy; it is a working animal whose performance
depends on training, maintenance, handler discipline and how the search is run. That is
why we publish the handler, the dog, the certifying body and the certification dates
instead of a percentage, and why no certification is claimed anywhere on this site until
those fields are real and checkable.

You can read how a search is actually conducted at
[how canine detection works](/how-canine-detection-works/).

## Working with the rest of Whatcom County

Bellingham is the largest market in the county but it is not the whole picture, and the
surrounding towns have conditions that Bellingham does not.

North and east of the city, the November 2021 Nooksack flooding remains the dominant
housing fact. Displacement, temporary accommodation, donated and replacement furniture and
repeated moves are all well-documented introduction pathways, and that is written up
honestly on the pages for [Everson](/service-area/everson/),
[Nooksack](/service-area/nooksack/) and [Sumas](/service-area/sumas/). West of the city,
[Ferndale](/service-area/ferndale/) runs on Cherry Point industrial employment and
contractor lodging that spikes around plant turnarounds. [Lynden](/service-area/lynden/)
has the county's agricultural labour housing, which is a distinct setting with its own
licensing and access rules and should never be treated as ordinary multifamily.
[Blaine](/service-area/blaine/) carries the border crossings and a vacation-rental base.

If you hold property across several of these, one schedule and one document format across
all of it is worth more than the sum of separate visits. That is the argument for a
programme rather than a call-out, and it is set out at

## What should happen in the first 48 hours after a tenant reports bites?

Slow down, and resist the urge to book treatment before anyone has established there is
something to treat.

Bites are not a diagnosis. Reactions vary enormously between people, a significant share
of people show no visible reaction at all, and plenty of other things bite or irritate
skin. A report of bites tells you that you have a complaint to resolve. It does not tell
you that you have an infestation. Treating it as though it does is how a manager pays for
work that was never needed and still has the original complaint at the end of it.

The sequence that tends to hold up later is this:

1. Record the report in writing, with the date and the unit, before anything else happens.
2. Ask what was seen rather than what was felt. Live insects, cast skins, eggs or dark
   staining along seams and bed frames are evidence. Bites on their own are not.
3. Serve entry notice properly under RCW 59.18.150 for whatever inspection follows. A
   rushed entry creates a second dispute alongside the first one.
4. Screen the reporting unit **and** a sensible set of neighbours, not the reporting unit
   alone. Which neighbours depends on how the building is built.
5. Only then decide whether treatment is warranted, and engage a licensed applicator of
   your own choosing if it is.

Step four is the one that gets skipped, and it is the step that decides whether you are
resolving a problem or following it around a building for a year. There is a dedicated
piece on the first part of this at
[bites but no bed bugs found](/services/k9-bed-bug-detection/bites-but-no-evidence/).
