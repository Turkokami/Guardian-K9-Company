## What does Seattle require that the state does not?

It names the pest, sets a programme standard, and attaches a daily price to getting it wrong.

Most of Washington regulates bed bugs only through the general habitability duty in
**RCW 59.18.060(4)**, which requires a landlord to provide a reasonable programme for the
control of infestation by insects, rodents and other pests. Bed bugs are covered by
implication, along with everything else.

Seattle is the exception. Under **SMC 22.206.160.A.3** and **SMC 22.206.170.C**, owners of
rental housing are required to control insects, and **bed bugs are named explicitly** rather
than left to inference. **SDCI Director's Rule 2-88** sets out what an extermination
programme is expected to look like and the verification procedures around it. Renters cannot
be charged for the work unless they caused the infestation. And the enforcement position is
concrete: penalties running from **$150 to $500 per day**, plus inspection charges.

Sitting underneath all of that is the **Rental Registration and Inspection Ordinance**,
which requires rental housing to be registered and subjects registered properties to
periodic inspection on a cycle.

The combined effect is that a Seattle rental portfolio is already inside an inspection
regime before any pest issue arises, and the pest issue itself has a named standard and a
meter running.

## Why does the daily accrual change the economics?

Because a per-day penalty converts delay into a variable cost, and variable costs compound
quietly.

A single fixed fine is an event. You absorb it, you fix the problem, you move on. A penalty
that accrues from $150 to $500 for every day the condition persists behaves completely
differently. Three weeks of slow scheduling, waiting on a vendor, arguing internally about
budget authority, or trying to establish whether the tenant caused it, is three weeks of
accrual on top of whatever the underlying remediation costs.

That reframes the value of speed. In most of Washington, the argument for responding quickly
to a bed bug report is about limiting spread, protecting the relationship with the resident,
and avoiding a habitability complaint. In Seattle, all of those still apply and there is also
a number attached to each day of hesitation.

It also reframes the value of knowing quickly. A large share of the delay in these situations
is not remediation, it is uncertainty: is there actually a problem, how far has it spread,
which units are affected. A screening pass that answers those questions in a day rather than
a fortnight removes the part of the timeline that is pure indecision.

## Who pays, and what has to be shown?

The owner, unless the tenant caused it. Which is a claim requiring evidence.

Seattle's position mirrors the state carve-out: renters cannot be charged unless they caused
the infestation. That sounds like a simple test and it is not, for the same reason it is not
simple under state law. Bed bugs arrive with people and belongings, they move between units
through shared assemblies, and finding them in a unit tells you nothing about where they
started.

An owner asserting tenant causation is making a factual claim and needs something behind it.
"The tenant must have brought them in" is an assumption about a common scenario rather than
evidence about this one. Given the penalty schedule and the inspection regime, an owner who
pursues a chargeback on an assumption and gets it wrong has spent money and time in a
jurisdiction where both are expensive.

What actually helps is a dated record of which units were searched, on what date, what was
confirmed and what was clear. That establishes distribution and timing. It does not establish
origin, because no inspection method can, and any provider claiming otherwise is overselling.
The general position is at
[who is responsible under Washington landlord-tenant law](/compliance/washington-landlord-tenant-bed-bugs/).

## What does the market context look like?

A majority-renter city with a rising national ranking, which is worth reading carefully.

Seattle has roughly 784,777 residents across about 363,466 households, with owner-occupancy
around 43.7 per cent. That puts renters at approximately 56 per cent of households, on the
order of 205,000 renter households. Those are Census figures and they should be treated as
approximations rather than precise counts.

Orkin placed Seattle **30th on its 2026 bed bug cities list, up from 39th the year before**.
That figure gets quoted widely and is usually quoted wrong. It ranks metropolitan areas by
Orkin's own treatment volume, not by scientific incidence. It is a demand signal from one
company's book of business, and a rise in the ranking may reflect greater awareness and more
treatment purchasing as much as anything about the underlying insect population. It is worth
knowing and it is not epidemiology, and we attribute it accordingly.

What is more directly useful for planning is that a majority-renter city with a large
pre-1940 apartment stock has a lot of shared-wall construction, which is what makes
adjoining-unit screening the norm here rather than the exception.

## How does the inspection culture change the response?

It raises the baseline expectation of documentation for everyone involved.

Where a jurisdiction subjects rentals to periodic inspection, owners become accustomed to
producing records on request, to third parties walking their buildings, and to a compliance
file that somebody outside the company may read. That is not a burden peculiar to Seattle.
Bellingham operates a rental registration and safety inspection programme under BMC 6.15
built around life and fire safety, and owners there show the same familiarity.

The consequence for pest documentation is that a dated third-party inspection report lands
in an existing filing habit rather than arriving as an unfamiliar object that has to be
explained before it can be used. In jurisdictions with no inspection culture at all, the
same document frequently sits in somebody's email until it is needed and then cannot be
found.

There is a governance dimension too. Where a penalty schedule exists, an owner's ability to
show a prompt, documented, reasonable response is directly financially relevant rather than
merely prudent.

## What does this mean for a multi-city portfolio?

That one policy across Washington is almost certainly wrong somewhere.

This is the practical failure we see most often in regional management. A company writes a
single pest response policy, usually calibrated to its largest or most demanding market, and
applies it across the portfolio.

Calibrate to Seattle and you over-document everywhere else. That is not harmful, only
expensive, and it tends to erode as staff notice the process is heavier than the local rules
require.

Calibrate to the northern counties and you under-document the Seattle assets, in a
jurisdiction with a named standard, a verification expectation and a daily penalty schedule.
That is the expensive direction.

The workable approach is a common documentation floor across the portfolio, set at whatever
the strictest jurisdiction requires for the artefacts that cost nothing to produce
consistently — dated records, named inspector, scope statement, results per unit — with the
jurisdiction-specific procedural obligations layered on top only where they apply. The
document format stays the same everywhere. The response obligations differ.

access constraint that governs multi-unit work statewide, see

## What does a verification standard change?

It makes "we treated it" an insufficient answer, which is the point.

Where a jurisdiction sets out programme standards and verification procedures, the
obligation stops being satisfied by the act of remediation alone. Somebody has to establish
afterwards that the condition is actually resolved, and an operator confirming its own work
has an obvious structural difficulty in doing that credibly.

This is the clearest case in the whole field for an independent check. The company that
performed the treatment has every ordinary professional reason to believe it worked, and a
commercial reason to conclude so. A separate party with no stake in the answer produces a
verification that a regulator, a tenant's advocate or an owner can actually rely on.

It also produces a cleaner file. A dated remediation record followed by a dated independent
verification, with the verification stating what was searched and what was found, closes an
episode. A remediation record with nothing after it leaves the episode open indefinitely,
and open episodes are what resurface eighteen months later.

That is what [post-treatment verification](/services/bed-bug-verification-inspection/) is
for, and it is a large share of the work we do in jurisdictions with an inspection culture.

## What does the older housing stock change?

The search takes longer, and adjoining-unit screening stops being optional.

Seattle carries a substantial pre-1940 apartment inventory alongside everything built since.
Pre-war brick courtyard buildings and subdivided older housing have trim, skirting, picture
rails, floor junctions and wall voids that offer far more harbourage than the sealed
assemblies of a podium mid-rise built in the last decade. There are more places to search,
each unit takes longer, and a scoping assumption borrowed from newer stock will be wrong.

More consequentially, shared-wall construction at that density makes screening only the
reporting unit close to pointless. An infestation found in one unit of a pre-war building is
a question about the building. Which neighbours to include depends on how the specific
building is put together rather than on the conventional grid of left, right, above and
below, and in buildings with shared voids and chases the relevant unit is frequently one
that grid would not have listed.

Newer construction is not lower risk, and it would be wrong to imply otherwise. Bed bugs
arrive with occupants, not with the building. What newer construction changes is how far
they can travel once they arrive and how quickly a room can be ruled out, which affects
scope and cost rather than likelihood.

## What does a defensible response timeline look like?

Fast at the front, documented throughout, and closed at the end.

**Day zero.** The report is received and logged in writing, with a date and a unit. Not a
verbal note. The date is the item everything later depends on.

**Day zero to one.** Establish what was actually observed rather than what was felt. Bites
are not a diagnosis; reactions vary enormously and plenty of other things irritate skin.
What matters is whether live insects, cast skins, eggs or dark faecal staining were seen.

**Day one.** Serve entry notice under RCW 59.18.150 for the reporting unit and for a
sensible set of neighbours. The two-day minimum starts running now, which is precisely why
this step should not wait for a budget conversation.

**Day three or four.** Screen. Reporting unit plus neighbours, with results recorded per
unit as confirmed, not confirmed or inconclusive, and with any unit that could not be entered
listed by number.

**After that.** If remediation is warranted, engage a licensed applicator of your own
choosing, and give them the alert map, which makes their scoping more accurate.

**At the end.** Verify independently, and keep the record.

The value of writing it out this way is that every step has a date attached, and a dated
sequence is what demonstrates a reasonable response in a jurisdiction where each day of
delay carries a number. It is also the sequence that costs least, because the expensive
version is the one where three weeks pass while people establish whether there is a problem
at all.

## What is most often misunderstood here?

Three things, and each of them costs somebody money.

**That the penalty schedule is the main risk.** It is the most quotable part and rarely the
largest number. The bigger exposure is usually the combination of vacancy loss, remediation
across more units than were originally affected, and the cost of a dispute that could have
been closed with a dated record. The penalty concentrates attention, which is useful, but a
manager optimising only against it is optimising against the smaller figure.

**That naming bed bugs makes Seattle uniquely strict.** It makes Seattle uniquely
*explicit*. The underlying habitability duty in RCW 59.18.060(4) applies statewide, and a
landlord in Bellingham or Mount Vernon who ignores an infestation is not in a comfortable
position simply because their city code does not use the words. What Seattle removes is the
argument about whether the duty covers this pest.

**That the tenant-causation carve-out is a practical route to recovery.** It exists in both
the state statute and the municipal position, and it requires evidence that is genuinely
difficult to assemble. Managers frequently plan around it as though it were the default.
Treating it as an exception you must prove, rather than an assumption you may rely on, leads
to better decisions and fewer reversed chargebacks.

The common thread is that the documentation habit is what converts each of these from an
argument into a record. That is the same conclusion the rest of this site keeps arriving at,
and it is not a coincidence: it is the only part of the process a property owner fully
controls.
