## What is the dog actually detecting?

Odour, not insects.

This distinction sounds pedantic and it governs everything else on this page. A detection
dog is not looking for bed bugs and is not finding them visually. It is trained to indicate
on a specific scent profile associated with live bed bugs and viable eggs, and to ignore
everything else in a room. The dog's advantage over a person is not better eyesight in dark
corners. It is that odour moves through voids, seams and fabric that no inspector can open,
and that a dog can sample an entire room faster than a person can examine one mattress
seam.

That is why canine screening scales and visual inspection does not. A trained team can
clear a large number of spaces in the time it takes a technician to properly examine a
handful, and the coverage is different in kind rather than merely faster.

It is also why the discrimination question matters so much. A dog trained loosely will
indicate on dead insects, shed skins and old faecal staining as readily as on a live
population. In a building with any treatment history those residues are widespread, and a
team that cannot tell them apart will produce alerts in units with no current problem.
Live insects and viable eggs are the targets worth paying for, because they are the only
ones that describe a situation you need to act on.

## What actually happens during an inspection?

A systematic search, then a slower confirmation pass, then the paperwork.

**Before we arrive**, we want to know what the space is, how many rooms are furnished, who
is admitting us, and whether anything has already been applied in the unit. That last one
is not a criticism. It changes what the search can show, and we would rather know than
discover it.

**The search itself** runs the perimeter of each room first, then the furniture, then the
specific high-probability assemblies: bed frames and headboards, upholstered seating,
seams, the junction of skirting and floor, and anywhere fabric meets a hard edge. The
handler directs the pattern and controls the pace. The dog works in short productive
stretches rather than continuously, because a tired dog is an unreliable dog and there is
no way to tell from the outside which one you are watching.

**When the dog indicates**, the search stops and the handler examines the location
directly, with a light and, where appropriate, by lifting or opening what can reasonably be
lifted or opened. This is the part that separates a report from a rumour. The handler is
looking for live insects, cast skins, eggs or the dark faecal staining that follows a
harbourage, and photographing what is there.

**The record** is written the same visit. Every alert location, the confirmation result for
each, photographs where confirmation was possible, and a clear statement of what was
searched and what was not.

## How long does it take, and what changes that?

Less time than people expect for the search, more time than people expect for everything
around it.

The search portion of a standard occupied one-bedroom is usually a matter of minutes. What
determines the length of the visit is everything else: access, confirmation on any alerts,
and the number of units in sequence.

Clutter is the single biggest variable, and it matters far more than floor area. A heavily
occupied studio with stored belongings under and around the bed can take longer than a
large, sparsely furnished apartment. Storage units, stacked boxes and furniture pushed
against walls all slow a search and, past a threshold, make parts of it impossible. Where
that happens we record the area as inconclusive rather than clear, because those two words
describe different situations and only one of them is true.

Other things that move the clock: pets that need securing, residents who understandably
want to talk while the dog is working, units where access was arranged with the wrong
person, and buildings where the lift is out. None of these are anybody's fault. All of them
are worth knowing about at the quoting stage rather than at four in the afternoon.

## What should you do before we come?

Very little, and specifically not the things people instinctively do.

Leave the space as it normally is. The instinct before an inspection is to clean
thoroughly, air the rooms out, launder bedding, strip the bed and move furniture around. It
is entirely understandable and it makes the search harder rather than easier. Vacuuming and
laundering remove some of what the handler would otherwise confirm visually. Airing a room
out disturbs the odour picture the dog is reading. Moving furniture relocates the evidence
away from the place that would have told us something.

The genuinely useful preparation is boring:

- **Secure pets** somewhere the dog will not meet them.
- **Make sure the rooms can be entered**, including any that are normally kept shut.
- **Have someone available** who can actually authorise access to every space on the list.
- **Say what you know.** If a resident has reported bites, if a previous inspection
  happened, if anything has been applied in the space, tell us in advance.
- **Do not strip the beds.** Bedding in its normal state is more informative than bedding in
  a laundry basket.

If you have already had work done in the unit, that does not make an inspection pointless.
It changes what it means. Residual scent sources can persist after a population is gone,
which is one of several reasons a post-treatment check is a different exercise from a first
inspection. That is covered at
[post-treatment verification](/services/bed-bug-verification-inspection/).

## What are people usually wrong about before we arrive?

Three things, consistently, and each one costs somebody money.

**Bites are not a diagnosis.** Reactions vary enormously between individuals, a substantial
share of people show no visible reaction at all, and a long list of other things bite or
irritate skin. A bite report tells you there is a complaint to resolve. It does not tell
you what is causing it. There is a whole page on this at
[bites but no bed bugs found](/services/k9-bed-bug-detection/bites-but-no-evidence/).

**Cleanliness is not the variable.** Bed bugs arrive with people and belongings, not with
poor housekeeping. They appear in immaculate homes and in new buildings. The persistent
belief otherwise is the main reason residents delay reporting, which is the actual thing
that makes an infestation expensive. If your internal messaging implies that a report
reflects badly on the resident, you have built a delay into your own process.

**Newer construction is not lower risk.** Sealed modern assemblies change where an
infestation can spread and how quickly a room can be cleared, which affects scoping. They
say nothing about whether one arrives. Older wood-frame stock offers more harbourage in
trim, skirting and wall voids, and takes longer to search properly, which is a cost
question rather than a probability one.

## When should you inspect rather than wait?

At the moments where finding something is cheapest, which are rarely the moments when
somebody complains.

**At turnover**, between tenancies, when the unit is empty and accessible and nobody's
belongings are in the way. This is the least expensive inspection you will ever buy and the
most useful, because it establishes a dated baseline for the unit at a known point. See
[move-in and move-out screening](/services/turnover-screening/).

**When a neighbouring unit is confirmed.** One confirmed unit in a multi-unit building is a
question about the building, not about the unit. Which neighbours to screen depends on the
construction rather than on the conventional diagram, and is set out at
[adjoining unit screening](/services/k9-bed-bug-detection/adjoining-unit-screening/).

**After somebody else has treated**, to establish independently whether the work achieved
what it was meant to. An operator verifying its own work has an obvious difficulty here.

**Before second-hand furniture enters a building.** Screening an item before it goes in is
dramatically cheaper than dealing with what follows if it should not have.
[Screening used furniture](/services/k9-bed-bug-detection/used-furniture-screening/)
covers the detail.

**After a period of high transient occupancy**, which for many of our clients means after a
season rather than after a complaint.

## What do you do with the report afterwards?

File it, act on the confirmed findings, and do not throw away the unconfirmed ones.

The confirmed alerts tell you where to direct a licensed applicator of your own choosing.
We will not recommend a method, a product or a company, because that is separately licensed
work in Washington and we do not hold that licence. What we will do is give whoever you
engage a precise map of where the problem was found, which makes their job smaller and
their quote more accurate.

The unconfirmed alerts are not noise and should not be discarded. They may reflect residual
odour from a past problem, a scent source in an adjoining void, or the dog being wrong.
Where a unit produces repeated unconfirmed alerts across visits, that pattern is
information you would not otherwise have.

The all-clears matter most in the long run, which surprises people. A dated document
stating that a unit was searched on a specific date and nothing was found is the thing that
answers a question asked eighteen months later about what you knew and when. We retain the
file so it can be reissued, because the request nearly always arrives long after anyone
remembers the visit. The standard we hold that document to is at
[what makes a report defensible](/compliance/what-a-defensible-report-contains/).

## What is the handler looking for at an alert?

Four categories of physical evidence, in roughly the order they get found.

**Live insects.** The unambiguous result, and the least common thing to see immediately. An
adult is about the size of an apple seed, flattened, reddish-brown, and rounder and darker
after feeding. Immature stages are smaller, paler and genuinely hard to see against a light
fabric. Finding a live insect at the alert location closes the question.

**Cast skins.** Bed bugs shed as they grow through their immature stages, and the discarded
skins persist long after the insect has gone. They look like translucent hollow shells of
the insect itself. They are strong evidence that a population was breeding rather than that
a single traveller was carried in, because shedding implies growth.

**Eggs.** Small, pale, and cemented to the surface rather than loose, usually tucked into a
seam or crevice. Viable eggs matter because they are part of what the dog is trained to
indicate on and because they determine whether a problem is finished or paused.

**Faecal staining.** The most commonly found evidence in practice. Dark spots that have
bled slightly into fabric, rather than sitting on top of it like a crumb, clustered along
seams, at the junction of skirting and floor, behind headboards and in the folds of
upholstery. Clusters indicate a harbourage nearby rather than a passing insect.

Where the handler is looking for these is as important as what they are. Bed frames and
headboards, the seams and piping of mattresses, upholstered seating and its underside, the
gap where skirting meets flooring, curtain hems, the backs of picture frames and any place
fabric meets a hard edge or a void. In older wood-frame stock there is far more of this
than in newer construction, which is why the same nominal unit size can take substantially
longer to search in a pre-war building.

What does not count as confirmation is worth stating too. A bite is not evidence, because
it is a reaction and reactions vary. A single unidentified insect on a floor is not
evidence until it has been identified. A resident's account of what they saw last week is
useful context and is not a finding. Nothing enters the report as confirmed unless it was
seen, and where possible photographed, at the location the dog indicated on.

If confirmation is not possible because the location cannot be reached or opened, that is
recorded as inconclusive. It is the least satisfying line in any report and it is the one
that keeps the rest of the document trustworthy.

## What drives the price?

**Unit count and total area**, which set the search time. **Access conditions**, which
determine how much of the day is spent searching rather than waiting. **Travel**, which is

## Does the time of year matter?

Less than for most pests, and not in the way people assume.

Bed bugs live indoors in heated buildings, so they are not knocked back by a wet
Washington winter the way an outdoor insect is. There is no off-season during which a
building is safe and no month in which an inspection is pointless. Anyone describing this
as seasonal work is describing a different pest.

What is genuinely seasonal is everything around the insect. Travel peaks, university
move-in and move-out weeks, festival and visitor seasons, and the turnover cycle of
short-stay accommodation all cluster introduction opportunities into predictable parts of
the year. Those are the patterns worth scheduling against, and they differ by market: an
Anacortes refinery turnaround, a Skagit April, a Bellingham September and a cruise season
are four different calendars.

The practical version is that the best time to screen is determined by your building's
occupancy rhythm rather than by the weather. Screening just after a high-turnover period
finds things while they are still small and cheap.

## What this inspection cannot tell you

Three limits, stated plainly, because they are the ones that get glossed over.

It cannot prove absence. A written all-clear says what was searched on a date and that
nothing was found. It does not certify the building is free of bed bugs, and no screening
method can.

It cannot establish who brought them in. A dog does not do origin. What a programme gives
you is distribution and timing, which is the material a causation argument is built from
rather than the argument itself.

And it is not uniformly reliable across every team in the industry. The published field
research shows an enormous spread between certified teams, which is why we publish the
handler, the dog, the certifying body and the dates rather than a percentage, and why the
full numbers live at [detection accuracy](/detection-accuracy/) instead of being reshaped
into a sales line here.

If a screening programme is what you need, tell us the property type, the unit mix and the
construction, and we will scope it. If a visual inspection of one room would answer your
question faster, we will tell you that instead.
