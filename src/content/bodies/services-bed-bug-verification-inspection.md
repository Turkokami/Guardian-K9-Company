## What is verification actually for?

Closing a file, rather than opening one.

An inspection answers "is there a problem." A verification answers a different and
under-served question: "is the problem you paid to remove actually gone." Those sound like
the same search and they are not, because the second one arrives loaded with an expectation
that everybody in the room has a stake in.

The applicator expects it to be clear, because they treated it. The property manager expects
it to be clear, because they paid and because the resident has been waiting. The resident may
expect the opposite, because they are still uneasy. And the physical evidence in a recently
treated space is genuinely harder to read than in an untreated one.

That combination is why this is the point in the sequence most likely to produce a
comfortable answer rather than an accurate one, and why it is the point where independence
is worth the most.

## Why can a treatment company not simply verify itself?

Because it is the party being assessed, and the assessment has consequences for it.

Nobody needs to be dishonest for this to matter. Consider the position of a technician
re-entering a unit they treated three weeks ago. They find something ambiguous: dark staining
in a seam that may be old faecal marking from the original infestation or may be recent. A
finding means the treatment failed, a callback at their own cost, an awkward conversation,
and a mark against a job they believed they did well. A clear result means the file closes.

That is an enormous amount of pressure to put on a marginal judgement, repeated across
hundreds of jobs a year. The distribution of those calls bends without anyone deciding to
bend it. It is the same structural argument that applies to inspection generally, set out at
[why independence is the product](/independent-detection/), and it applies with more force
here, because at verification the company is not merely selling the next step. It is grading
its own homework.

Guardian did not perform the treatment. We do not sell treatment. We take no referral fee
from whoever did. A clear result costs us nothing and a finding earns us nothing, which is
the only arrangement under which the answer means anything.

## When should verification happen?

After the treatment has finished doing whatever it does, which is a question for the
applicator rather than for us.

Different approaches have different timelines and different assumptions about surviving eggs.
Some protocols expect a follow-up cycle. Some assume a period during which any eggs present
at treatment will hatch and encounter residual conditions. Verifying before that window has
closed produces a result describing an incomplete process rather than an outcome.

We will not advise you on that window, and it is worth saying why rather than sounding
evasive. Under **RCW 15.58.210**, offering technical advice or making recommendations to the
user of a pesticide is the work of a licensed pest control consultant, and Guardian does not
hold that licence. Asking the applicator what their protocol assumes is both the correct
route and the more reliable one, because they know what they applied and we do not.

What we can say is the scheduling implication. Book the verification when you book the
treatment, for a date after that window. Verification arranged retrospectively, weeks after
everyone has moved on, tends to happen only when something has already resurfaced, by which
point it is an inspection again rather than a verification.

## Why is a treated space harder to search?

Because the evidence of a past infestation does not leave when the infestation does.

Cast skins persist. Faecal staining persists. Dead insects remain in voids nobody can reach.
Some of what a detection dog indicates on can linger in a space where no living population
remains, and in a unit with a long treatment history those residues can be widespread.

That has three consequences a verification report has to handle honestly.

**An alert is not a failure.** In a treated space an alert with no visual confirmation is
common, and reading it as proof the treatment did not work would be wrong.

**A confirmation needs care.** Finding cast skins or old staining confirms something was
present. Establishing something is present *now* is a different claim, and it turns on
whether there is live evidence rather than historical residue.

**Discrimination matters more here than anywhere.** A team that indicates readily on dead
insects and old residues will light up in every unit with a history, which makes it useless
for exactly this job. Live insects and viable eggs are the targets worth paying for.

None of that is a reason to skip verification. It is a reason to insist the report records
unconfirmed alerts as unconfirmed rather than resolving them in whichever direction suits
the reader.

## What does a verification report say?

The same three states as any other search, applied to a harder question.

**Confirmed** means live insects, cast skins, eggs or faecal staining were seen at the alert
location and photographed where possible, with a note on whether the evidence was live or
historical.

**Not confirmed** means the dog alerted and nothing was visually found. In a treated space
this is the most frequent outcome at an alert, and it stays in the document.

**Inconclusive** means access or conditions prevented a proper look.

Where nothing is found anywhere, you get a written all-clear stating what was searched, what
was not searched, and the date. In verification that all-clear is the entire product. It is
what lets a manager tell a resident the unit was independently checked, lets an owner close a
file, and answers the question in eighteen months about whether the episode was resolved or
merely stopped being discussed.

The standard is at
[what makes a report defensible](/compliance/what-a-defensible-report-contains/).

## What happens if we find something?

We write down what we found. That is the whole of our role.

We do not tell you the treatment was inadequate, that the applicator used the wrong approach,
or that you should engage somebody else. We are not licensed to make the first two judgements
and we have no basis for the third. What you receive is a dated record of what was present on
the date we searched, which you can put in front of the applicator.

In practice that conversation goes better than people expect. A competent applicator handed a
precise alert map showing where live evidence remains has something actionable, and most
warranty and callback arrangements exist for exactly this situation. An independent record
often triggers the warranty cleanly rather than turning into an argument about whether the
problem is new.

Occasionally a verification finds a space in better condition than a resident's continuing
reports suggest. That result is just as useful and considerably harder to obtain from a party
with an interest, because it is the finding that ends an escalating complaint rather than
feeding it.

## Who commissions this?

Four groups, for three different reasons.

**Property managers**, closing out a unit before re-letting or before telling a resident the
matter is resolved. This is the largest share of the work, and it pairs naturally with
[turnover screening](/services/turnover-screening/) when the unit is empty anyway.

**Facilities and institutional buyers**, where an internal process requires remediation to be
evidenced rather than asserted. Hospitals, colleges, care settings and public bodies
generally cannot close a file on a contractor's own assurance.

**Hotel and lodging operators**, returning a room to inventory. A room out of service has a
nightly cost, and the decision to resell it is easier to defend with an independent record
than with a technician's word, particularly if the original complaint reached a review site.

**Owners and boards**, after a building-wide programme. Following remediation across multiple
units, verification across the property establishes whether the building is genuinely closed
out or whether a reservoir remains. That usually runs as a
[building-wide sweep](/services/building-wide-sweep/) rather than unit by unit.

## Where jurisdiction makes this more than good practice

In places with a defined programme standard, verification stops being optional hygiene.

Seattle names bed bugs explicitly in its housing code, and its Director's Rule sets out
extermination programme standards and verification procedures, with penalties running from
$150 to $500 per day plus inspection charges. Where a jurisdiction expects verification to
have happened, an owner relying solely on the applicator's own confirmation is relying on the
least independent evidence available. The detail is at
[Seattle rental bed bug rules](/compliance/seattle-rental-bed-bug-rules/).

Elsewhere in Washington the position is the general habitability duty under
**RCW 59.18.060(4)**, which requires a reasonable programme without defining one. That
vagueness is usually read as leniency. It is better read as an evidentiary standard: where
"reasonable" is undefined, what you have instead is a record of what you actually did, and a
remediation record followed by an independent verification is a far stronger account than a
remediation record alone. That is at
[who is responsible under Washington landlord-tenant law](/compliance/washington-landlord-tenant-bed-bugs/).

## How does this fit a multi-round programme?

Verification is a checkpoint rather than a single event, and in a building it repeats.

Where remediation runs across several units in waves, verifying each wave as it completes
gives you something a single verification at the end cannot: a sequence. If wave one verifies
clear and the same units alert again two months later, that is a different problem from wave
one never having cleared at all, and only a dated sequence distinguishes them.

That distinction matters because the two situations call for opposite responses. A unit that
never cleared points at the remediation. A unit that cleared and then failed points at
reintroduction, which is a question about adjoining units, shared assemblies and occupant
movement rather than about the treatment. Managers who verify only once at the end of a
programme routinely misdiagnose the second as the first, and spend money accordingly.

For buildings of any size, this is the argument for a cadence rather than a set of isolated
visits, which is set out at
[multifamily and property management](/commercial/multifamily/).

## What should you ask the applicator first?

Four questions, before the verification is even scheduled, because the answers set the date
and the scope.

**What does your protocol assume about follow-up?** Some approaches expect a second visit as
standard. Verifying between the first and second treatment measures an unfinished process,
which produces a result nobody can use.

**How long until the treatment has fully run its course?** This is the single item that
determines the verification date, and only the applicator can answer it, because it depends
on what they applied and how.

**Which spaces did you actually treat?** Not which spaces were in scope on the quote. Rooms
get skipped for legitimate reasons: no access, an occupant declined, conditions on the day.
If we verify a space that was never treated, a finding tells you nothing about the
remediation.

**What did you find, and where?** A map of the original findings makes the verification
sharper, because we know which locations carry a history and which do not. It changes how an
alert in a given spot is interpreted.

None of those is an awkward question to ask, and an applicator who answers all four readily
is usually one whose work verifies clean.

## Why the record outlasts the episode

Because the question this document answers is asked long after everybody has stopped
thinking about it.

The pattern is consistent. A unit has a problem, the problem is treated, the file closes, and
eighteen months later somebody asks what happened: a tenant's advocate during a dispute, a
purchaser during due diligence, an insurer after a claim, or a new manager inheriting a
building with a reputation. At that point nobody involved remembers the sequence, and the
only thing that survives is what was written down.

A remediation invoice on its own establishes that money was spent. It does not establish
that the problem went away. A verification record dated after the treatment, produced by a
party with no stake in the answer, does exactly that, and it does it in a form somebody
hostile can check rather than simply accept.

This is why we retain the file so it can be reissued, and why the all-clear is written to the
same standard as a finding. On the day it is issued it is the least interesting page in the
file. It is frequently the most valuable one two years later.

## What are the limits?

Four, and the first is the one people most want to argue with.

**It does not certify absence.** A clear verification says we searched on that date and found
nothing. It does not say the space is free of bed bugs. No method proves a negative, and
anybody issuing a certificate of eradication is selling past the evidence. Where the word
guarantee appears anywhere on this site it is a defined term covering our process rather than
your outcome, set out at [our guarantee](/our-guarantee/).

**It does not assess the treatment.** It assesses the space. Those overlap and they are not
identical, and the distinction keeps the report from claiming expertise it does not have.

**Detection varies between teams.** The published field research shows a wide spread between
certified canine teams working real infested apartments, which is why we publish the handler,
the dog, the certifying body and the certification dates rather than a percentage. The
figures and sources are at [detection accuracy](/detection-accuracy/).

**A treated space is harder to read.** That is a genuine limitation of verification as an
exercise rather than something we can engineer away, and a provider who never mentions it has
not thought about the problem.

If you want verification scoped, tell us how many spaces are involved, roughly when the
treatment was carried out, and what the applicator's protocol assumes about follow-up timing.
That last item usually determines the date more than our calendar does. The honest picture of
where we are fastest is at [where we work](/service-area/).

One final observation, offered because it runs against our own commercial interest. If your
applicator is competent, communicative and willing to be checked, most of your verifications
will come back clear, and you may reasonably conclude after a year that you are paying for
confirmation of something you already expected. That is a fair conclusion to reach, and the
right response is usually to verify a sample of jobs rather than all of them, not to stop
verifying entirely. The value of the practice is that it stays honest precisely because
nobody knows in advance which job will be the one that does not come back clear.
